# Narrative Synthesis — Project Governance, Temporality & Megaprojects (29 Papers)

> **Research question:** *How does misalignment between electoral cycles and megaproject
> timelines shape governance outcomes?*
> This synthesis reads twenty-nine papers as one corpus. It is a standalone companion to the
> combined comparison table, the CSV, the thematic-matrix JSON, and the per-paper raw
> extractions. Section numbering follows the extraction specification.

---

## 1. Overview — Corpus characteristics

The corpus comprises **twenty-nine unique papers spanning 2008–2025** (one duplicate upload of
Ika et al. 2025 de-counted). Twenty-two are peer-reviewed journal articles, concentrated in the
*International Journal of Project Management* (11), with clusters in the *Project Management
Journal* (3), *Project Leadership and Society* (2), and single contributions in *World
Development*, *Global Environmental Change*, *Policy and Society*, *California Management
Review*, *Studies of Transition States and Societies*, *Information and Organization*, and the
*Scandinavian Journal of Management*. The remainder are a book chapter (Müller 2009), a PMI
white paper (Denicol 2020), a conference paper (Araral & Hartley 2013), and a systematic-review
working paper (Pathak et al. 2023).

**Disciplinary spread** is wider than a pure project-management reading suggests. Alongside the
project-governance mainstream (Müller; Ahola et al.; Biesenthal & Wilden; Joslin & Müller;
Sainati et al.; Unterhitzenberger et al.; Bakhshi et al.), the set reaches into political science
and public policy (Araral & Hartley; Menga; Livert et al.; Esposito & Terlizzi; Delcayre &
Bourdin), organization and institutional theory (Biesenthal et al.; Qiu et al.; Dille et al.;
Beck et al.), and strategy/management (Gil & Beckman; Malherbe). **Geographically** the evidence
is global: the UK (Denicol; Bourne; Gil & Beckman), Canada (Brunet; Brunet & Choinière), China
(Qiu), Norway (Dille et al.), the Netherlands and Australia (Van Marrewijk et al.), Chile
(Livert), Italy and Tajikistan (Menga), Nigeria (Afieroho et al.), and France (Malherbe;
Delcayre & Bourdin).

**Method** is roughly balanced between conceptual/theory-building work (Müller; Ahola; Biesenthal
& Wilden; Biesenthal et al.; Turner; Winch & Sergeeva; Ika et al.) and empirical work — single
and comparative case studies (Van Marrewijk; Qiu; Dille; Brunet; Malherbe; Beck; Afieroho;
Brunet & Choinière), econometric panels (Livert), survey research (Joslin & Müller;
Unterhitzenberger et al.), discourse analysis (Menga), and three systematic reviews (Denicol;
Pathak et al.; Bakhshi et al.). The **temporal centre of gravity is recent**: eighteen of the
twenty-nine papers are from 2019 or later, and the six 2024–2025 papers (Beck; Afieroho; Brunet &
Choinière; Bakhshi; Gil & Beckman; Ika; Delcayre & Bourdin) are precisely the ones that push
hardest on temporality and political turnover — evidence that the RQ sits on a live and
accelerating research frontier.

## 2. Definitional Landscape — consensus vs. divergence

There is a **stable structural core, an explicit consensus that no universal definition exists,
and a clear historical drift from "control" to "adaptation."** The recurrent core reads governance
as *structure + process + roles + accountability*: Turner's (2009) formulation — governance
"provides the structure through which the objectives … are set, and the means of attaining … and
monitoring performance are determined" (carried by Biesenthal & Wilden and by Pathak et al.
through the Turner 2006 / APM 2019 definitions) — recurs almost verbatim in Sainati et al.
(p. 244), Qiu et al. (p. 427), Joslin & Müller (via Pinto 2014, p. 613), Brunet (via Klakegg et
al. 2008), Bourne et al. (p. 2), and the PMI (2021) / Müller (2016) definitions consolidated by
Bakhshi et al. (2025).

Yet the field openly disclaims consensus. Ahola et al. (2014, p. 1324): "there is clearly a lack
of a shared and universally accepted view of what project governance actually is." Their
**external/internal** distinction — governance *external to* a project (a parent institution
imposing standards) versus *internal to* it (coordination among the delivering firms) — remains
the best organizing cut, and it is the axis Pathak et al. (2023) and Bakhshi et al. (2025)
inherit. Bakhshi et al. add the field's most authoritative recent framing: project governance
has moved "from control to adaptation."

Several papers refine the core in ways the RQ needs. **Turner (2020)** separates governance
(structure) from *governmentality* (agency — "the way governors interact with those they
govern"). **Sanderson (2012)** separates *governance* (ex-ante designed order) from *governing*
(ex-post spontaneous order). **Joslin & Müller (2016)** locate governance on two axes —
shareholder↔stakeholder orientation and behavior↔outcome control — and find success rises with
*stakeholder* orientation. **Bourne et al. (2023)** reconceive governance as something that must
*move* ("moving goals and moving governance"). **Unterhitzenberger et al. (2023)** add a
*vertical* refinement — a three-tier stack of **metagovernance / governance of networks / network
governance** whose top layer is "set by … public government organizations." **Afieroho et al.
(2024)** recast governance as governmentality/meta-organizing — the state steering
multi-stakeholder arenas.

A final group *declines* a governance definition to substitute another object: project design and
cultures (Van Marrewijk et al.), institutional logics (Biesenthal et al.; Qiu), time-reckoning
systems (Dille et al.), narrative and time (Winch & Sergeeva; Ika et al.), distributive
allocation (Livert), ideology (Menga), polycentric coordination (Araral & Hartley), coupling
(Malherbe), boundary work (Beck et al.), the governance trap between mandated inclusion and
shareholder-centric investment (Gil & Beckman), and wicked-policy-field theory (Esposito &
Terlizzi). **Fifteen of twenty-nine give an explicit definition; the rest work by substitution.**
The operative meaning of "project governance" now spans a control architecture, a
structure-plus-agency system, an ex-ante/ex-post process, a *moving* control system, a vertical
metagovernance stack, a governmentality practice, a shareholder/stakeholder balance, and a
temporally-constituted phenomenon — and the electoral-vs-project problem lives where these
readings overlap.

## 3. Governance Elements Mapping — core components across papers

A **four-element spine holds across all twenty-nine**: (i) structures/roles/accountabilities;
(ii) decision-making processes; (iii) multi-level / cross-organizational structure; (iv)
alignment/control mechanisms. Each paper thickens one or more strands. Van Marrewijk et al. (2008)
add **project design** (scope + risk allocation) and **project cultures**, plus the
turnkey/concession-vs-alliance distinction later formalized by Sanderson and Pathak et al. Joslin
& Müller (2016) add the **shareholder↔stakeholder / behavior↔outcome grid** and the
agency-vs-stewardship choice. Dille et al. (2018) add **timing norms** (sequence, duration,
rhythm, frequency) and three coping strategies (temporal avoidance/splitting/matching). Bourne et
al. (2023) add **alignment-over-time** and six systemic errors (design, experience, bias, phase
change, drift, intervention). Unterhitzenberger et al. (2023) add the **vertical metagovernance
stack** and the Type I / Type II (hierarchical / non-hierarchical) split. Pathak et al. (2023)
consolidate the inventory into **aspects / mechanisms / enablers** (formal + informal mechanisms;
leadership, collaboration, stakeholder management, culture as enablers). Brunet & Choinière (2024)
add **institutional layering and path dependency**; Afieroho et al. (2024) add **meta-organizing
and responsibilization**; Gil & Beckman (2025) add the **mandated-inclusion × shareholder-centricity
grid**; Bakhshi et al. (2025) add the **theory landscape** and name "adaptive/flexible governance"
and "temporal governance" as emerging elements. Earlier additions persist: Müller's capability
maturity; Turner's psychological constructs and OECD quartet; Brunet's
efficiency–legitimacy–accountability triangle; Denicol's role taxonomy; Araral & Hartley's
polycentric primitives; Esposito & Terlizzi's six-fold complexity; Qiu's four mechanisms.

The **multi-level / cross-organizational** element is the densest and most RQ-relevant: Ahola's
inter-firm nexus, Qiu's four-government HZMB, Denicol's multilayer client, Unterhitzenberger's
three-tier stack, Pathak et al.'s institution/organisation/project levels, Esposito & Terlizzi's
"supranational, national, regional, and municipal" complexity, Dille's three-directorate
inter-institutional split, Araral & Hartley's polycentric jurisdictions, and Afieroho's
federal/state/local tiers all describe governance stretched across levels — the substrate on
which electoral misalignment acts. Three reviews (Qiu p. 427; Pathak et al.; Bakhshi et al.)
explicitly flag the under-use of institutional, adaptive, and *temporal* lenses.

## 4. Stakeholder & Decision-Making Patterns — positioning and authority

All twenty-nine converge on a **multi-actor, conflict-laden** picture that runs *contained →
contested → politically-mediated → state-steered.* **Contained:** Sainati's SPV concentrates
control in lenders; Müller's and Ahola's sponsor/PM agency problem; Joslin & Müller's
parent-over-project control choice; Unterhitzenberger's Type I hierarchy with "clear roles …
distinct accountabilities." **Mid-spectrum:** Turner's multiple governmentalities, Sanderson's
three logics, Denicol's evolving role interfaces, Van Marrewijk's alliance/no-blame vs concession
cultures, Pathak et al.'s "no actor has total control and authority over all others," and Bourne
et al.'s client/contractor mandate-misalignments (a £5m change "should be taken a few levels up"
on the client side yet decided directly on the contractor side, p. 5). Beck et al.'s 120-agency
collaboration resolves conflict through deference to expertise and a shared boundary object;
Araral & Hartley's polycentric actors coordinate through mutual adjustment. **Contested:**
Biesenthal et al., Qiu, and Dille et al. treat conflict as constitutive — Dille's Health, Fire,
and Police subprojects fight over speeding-up versus slowing-down; Esposito & Terlizzi and Gil &
Beckman formalize external conflict (governments, citizens, movements, investors) that can "stop,
suspend, delay, or amend" a megaproject (Esposito & Terlizzi, p. 138) or trap it in "protracted
bargaining cycles" (Gil & Beckman, p. 7).

At the **political / state-steered** end sit the Cluster-D papers plus several others. Livert et
al. (2019) show central government disbursing transfers "to win, exercise, and retain power"
(p. 2). Menga (2018) shows the deciders themselves changing with elections (the Messina Bridge
assigned, cancelled, revived, cancelled across cabinets, p. 8). Van Marrewijk et al. (2008) show
politicians *taking over* Environ's governance after an €800m overrun and a parliamentary inquiry.
Gil & Beckman (2025) show a head of government reversing a flagship megaproject outright — Sunak's
HS2 phase-2 cancellation, "The facts have changed" (p. 6) — and contrast democratic delay with
authoritarian speed. Afieroho et al. (2024) put the government in the explicit "steering role" as
the dominant stakeholder, documenting a two-year delay "because the federal and state governments
were controlled by different political parties at the time" (p. 5). Brunet & Choinière (2024)
show the *governance framework's* actor set expanding and contracting with each change of
government. Delcayre & Bourdin (2025) recast local officials as political mediators timing their
positions to the electoral calendar.

The decision-making pattern for the RQ is decisive: **who decides is never neutral; the deciders
and their governmentality change mid-project (Turner's Amsterdam, Denicol's evolving sponsor, Van
Marrewijk's ministry takeover, Brunet's years-long turnover, Bourne's UK hospital programme
imposing new governance on in-flight projects); and in Clusters C–D that change is explicitly
driven by elections, partisan control, or government alternation.**

## 5. Temporality in Governance — count, themes, findings

**Twenty-three of twenty-nine papers discuss temporality substantively (Yes); four do so briefly
(Van Marrewijk; Araral & Hartley; Biesenthal & Wilden; Turner); two do not (Joslin & Müller 2016,
a cross-sectional snapshot; Sainati et al. 2020, SPV liquidation only).** The temporal material
clusters into five themes.

**(a) Dedicated temporality theory.** Ika, Söderlund & Pinto (2025) is the conceptual engine —
entrainment ("temporal fit between project activities and temporal demands imposed by the external
environment," pp. 5–6, explicitly including "politics, government regulations"), three time
streams (clock/event/process), timing norms, temporal fit/misfit, and divergent manager-vs-funder
horizons (p. 2). **Dille et al. (2018)** is the empirical keystone: *temporal conditioning* —
"the process of responding to and coping with institutionally prescribed and conflicting temporal
demands" (p. 673) — with three colliding institutional time-reckoning systems producing
"significant temporal misfit across the public subprojects" (p. 678), managed through temporal
avoidance/splitting/matching. Crucially, its external temporal gate is a **government/Parliament
funding decision** (Step 1→Step 2) — a concrete model of an exogenous political clock the project
must entrain to. Winch & Sergeeva (2022) add narrative re-projection across the shaping→delivery
life cycle.

**(b) Governance that must move over time.** Bourne et al. (2023) theorize that "the governance
needs to change as the requirements of the project change … over the extended length of
megaprojects," name "political pressures … over the life of the megaproject" (p. 8), and supply
six systemic errors — of which *drift* ("the project context changes and the governance approach
does not adapt"), *phase change*, and *intervention* ("get a grip" pressure) are ready-made
categories for electoral-misfit failures. Pathak et al. (2023) confirm at field level that
governance is "dynamic, processual and anchored in multiple levels" and that "governance regimes
must adapt … and change as the project development process unfolds" (p. 9). Bakhshi et al. (2025)
name "temporal governance" and "sociopolitical changes as critical triggers for governance
adaptations" as "often inadequately addressed."

**(c) The temporality *of governance itself*.** Ahola et al. (2014) surface the continuous-vs-
static debate; Sanderson operationalizes it (governing vs governance); Denicol (2020), Pathak et
al. (2023), and Unterhitzenberger et al. (2023) show roles/levels evolving over the life cycle;
Brunet & Choinière (2024) show the *framework* evolving across four decades and five governments.

**(d) Governance → time-horizon compression.** Müller (2009) and Sanderson (2012, p. 436, the
promoter-out-of-office gap) show pressure and political tenure collapsing the decision horizon.

**(e) Electoral / external temporality.** Biesenthal et al. (2018) name the duration-vs-political-
cycle tension (p. 45); Menga (2018) shows alternation-vs-continuity determining megaproject fate;
Livert (2019) specifies the electoral budget cycle; Afieroho (2024) shows partisan misalignment
delaying a megaproject; Brunet & Choinière (2024) map framework re-layering to government changes;
Gil & Beckman (2025) show a head-of-government cancellation and a regime-tempo contrast; Delcayre
& Bourdin (2025) state the mechanism outright.

## 6. Temporal Misalignment Discussion — count and examples

**Thirteen of twenty-nine papers explicitly discuss temporal mismatch/misalignment; eight of
those discuss an electoral, partisan, or political-turnover cycle in their own analysis**
(Sanderson; Menga; Biesenthal et al. 2018; Livert; Afieroho; Brunet & Choinière; Gil & Beckman;
Delcayre & Bourdin). The single most direct statement of the RQ's mechanism is Delcayre & Bourdin
(2025, p. 6): "The benefits of energy transition programmes generally materialise in the medium to
long term, whereas electoral mandates are short-lived. This temporal misalignment reinforces both
NIMEY and NIMTOO behaviours." Their **NIMEY** ("Not In My Electoral Year") typology is the one
purpose-built template in the corpus for the electoral-timeline mismatch — but it is developed for
local officials and renewable-energy projects, not $1bn megaprojects.

The other misalignment demonstrations each capture a *fragment*. Sanderson (2012, p. 436) names
the accountability gap — promoters "often not in office when the actual viability of a project can
be assessed." Menga (2018, p. 8) shows electoral alternation (Italy) versus authoritarian
continuity (Tajikistan) shaping two megaprojects' fates, but through *ideology/symbolism*.
Afieroho et al. (2024, p. 5) show a two-year delay from cross-level *partisan* misalignment, but
through *governmentality/community engagement*. Brunet & Choinière (2024, p. 84) show governance
frameworks re-layering "following the changes in the government parties," but at *framework* scale
and through *institutional layering / path dependency*, not a single project's timeline. Gil &
Beckman (2025, p. 6) show political funding reversals (HS2; California HSR) and a democracy-vs-
authoritarian tempo gap, but frame delay as *stakeholder bargaining*, not electoral entrainment.
The remaining misalignment papers (Dille — divergent institutional clocks with a Parliament gate;
Bourne — generic "moving governance"; Esposito & Terlizzi — policy-time can "delay or amend"; Ika
— entrainment/misfit; Bakhshi — field-level flag) supply the *machinery* while keeping the
external clock generic or one-off.

## 7. Megaproject Context — coverage of complex, multi-stakeholder projects

**Megaproject coverage is Full in 16 of 29, Partial in 6, and absent in 7 (Full+Partial =
22/29).** The sixteen explicitly megaproject-centred papers are Van Marrewijk et al. (2008),
Sanderson (2012), Menga (2018), Biesenthal et al. (2018), Dille et al. (2018, inter-institutional
large-scale), Qiu et al. (2019), Denicol (2020), Sainati et al. (2020), Brunet (2021), Bourne et
al. (2023), Esposito & Terlizzi (2023), Pathak et al. (2023, a megaproject-governance systematic
review), Unterhitzenberger et al. (2023), Afieroho et al. (2024), Brunet & Choinière (2024), and
Gil & Beckman (2025). The **case evidence is directly RQ-exposed**: Bourne's 24-year Swedish
windfarm, Dutch road programme, and UK "national priority" hospital programme exhibit governance
moving over multi-year, politically-exposed timelines; Van Marrewijk's Environ/NSTP and Gil &
Beckman's HS2 and California HSR show politics reshaping megaprojects mid-flight; Afieroho's
Lekki-Epe PPP (30-year concession) stalls on cross-level partisan conflict; Brunet & Choinière
situate megaproject governance inside four decades of government policy; Qiu's HZMB spans four
governments. Sectorally the set is dominated by transport/infrastructure and PPP, with energy
(Delcayre & Bourdin), water/environment (Araral & Hartley), and emergency-services technology
(Dille) at the edges.

The seven papers with **no megaproject framing** cover inter-organizational or multi-level
governance generically (Müller; Ahola; Joslin & Müller; Turner; Livert; Malherbe; Beck; Delcayre &
Bourdin) — valuable for mechanism but not context. The upshot: the megaproject *context* is very
well covered, *multi-level coordination* is strongly covered, and the electoral *mechanism* is
covered at several scales — but no single paper covers the megaproject context, the electoral
mechanism, and the temporal machinery **at once**.

## 8. Research Gap Analysis — what is NOT addressed

Reading all twenty-nine together yields a gap that is "demonstrated in fragments, never assembled
at megaproject-governance scale." Seven specific openings follow.

1. **The mechanism is empirically real at multiple scales but never integrated.** Delcayre &
   Bourdin (local officials, NIMEY), Menga (national megaproject fate), Gil & Beckman
   (head-of-government cancellation / regime tempo), Afieroho (cross-level partisan delay), and
   Brunet & Choinière (framework re-layering per government) each show a *piece*; none models the
   internal governance mechanics of one multi-year megaproject crossing several electoral cycles.

2. **A "moving governance" theory and a "temporal conditioning" theory now exist, but their clocks
   are generic or one-off.** Bourne et al. (2023) supply moving goals/governance and six systemic
   errors; Dille et al. (2018) supply the coping strategies (avoidance/splitting/matching) and a
   concrete government/Parliament gate; Bakhshi et al. (2025) confirm "temporal governance … often
   inadequately addressed." Specializing these to a *recurring electoral* clock is open work.

3. **The two clocks are studied apart.** Livert, Menga, Afieroho, Brunet & Choinière, Gil &
   Beckman, and Delcayre & Bourdin own the political/electoral clock; Qiu, Turner, Brunet, Denicol,
   Sanderson, Dille, Winch & Sergeeva, Bourne, Pathak et al., Unterhitzenberger, and Esposito &
   Terlizzi own the project/framework clock. Integrating the two is the contribution space.

4. **A ready-made temporal apparatus is pointed at elections only in Cluster D.** Ika
   (entrainment/misfit), Dille (temporal conditioning), Winch & Sergeeva (re-narration), and
   Bourne (moving governance) supply the machinery; the thesis can model the electoral cycle as an
   external *zeitgeber* the megaproject fails to entrain to — each turnover causing governance
   drift, an "intervention" reflex, temporal splitting/matching, and forced re-narration.

5. **The vertical governance stack localizes where an election bites.** Unterhitzenberger et al.'s
   (2023) *metagovernance* — "set by … public government organizations" — is precisely the layer an
   electoral change perturbs; Turner's governmentality, Denicol's government-sponsor node, and
   Joslin & Müller's shareholder↔stakeholder swing say the same at the agency/role/orientation
   level. The friction between a *durable* lower structure (network governance, SPVs, contracts)
   and a *cyclically-changing metagovernance/governmentality* is a crisp, novel formulation.

6. **Governance is modelled as static, endogenously-evolving, layering, or singly-governed — the
   recurring-external-clock case is unbuilt.** Sainati (static), Biesenthal & Wilden (fixed
   horizons), and Joslin & Müller (cross-sectional) at one end; Bourne (moving), Dille (temporal
   conditioning), Brunet & Choinière (layering across governments), Turner (restructuring), Denicol
   (evolving roles), Pathak et al. (evolutionary governance), and Araral & Hartley (polycentric)
   show governance *changing* — but none imposes a *fixed-cadence external clock* that changes the
   principals repeatedly, which is what elections do.

7. **Existing conflict/complexity/error typologies lack a temporal-electoral category — except
   NIMEY.** Qiu's six complexity types, Esposito & Terlizzi's six, Bourne's six systemic errors,
   Gil & Beckman's governance-trap quadrants, and Biesenthal et al.'s three pillars have no
   electoral-timing category; Delcayre & Bourdin's NIMEY/NIMTOO is the one template that does,
   built for local politics. The study can adapt and scale it — potentially as an
   "electoral-temporal" complexity type or a seventh systemic error ("electoral drift") for
   megaprojects.

**Bottom line.** The twenty-nine papers supply, between them, every component the research
question needs: a stable structural definition plus the external/internal, structure/governmentality,
governance/governing, and shareholder/stakeholder distinctions; a *moving-governance* theory
(Bourne) and a *temporal-conditioning* theory with a concrete political gate (Dille); a vertical
metagovernance stack whose top layer is government-set (Unterhitzenberger); institutional-logics,
complexity, and wicked-policy-field frames (Biesenthal et al.; Qiu; Esposito & Terlizzi; Pathak et
al.); a dedicated temporal apparatus (Ika; Winch & Sergeeva) and a field-level flag that
temporal/sociopolitical governance is under-theorized (Bakhshi et al.); a dozen-plus megaproject
cases including government-policy frameworks, PPP and high-speed-rail cases, and long-duration
politically-exposed cases; and eight empirical demonstrations of political-time or electoral
effects on projects. What none of them delivers — and what the thesis is positioned to build — is
the **integration at megaproject-governance scale**: a governance account in which an exogenous
electoral cycle, turning over the deciders, the government sponsor, the metagovernance layer, and
their governmentality repeatedly across a megaproject's multi-year, multi-electoral-term timeline,
becomes a first-class driver of governance outcomes through recurrent temporal misfit, governance
drift/intervention, temporal splitting/matching, forced re-narration, and friction between durable
structure and cyclical governmentality. The corpus proves the mechanism is real in fragments and
supplies every piece of the frame; none assembles them for megaproject governance. That assembly
is the study's contribution.
