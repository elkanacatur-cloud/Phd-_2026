# Entrainment — Separate Extraction from Two Papers

> Extraction only. The two papers are treated strictly separately; no comparison
> or synthesis is made here. All quotations are verbatim from the source PDFs.
> Page numbers refer to the printed page numbers shown in each document.

---

## Paper 1 — Ancona & Chong (1992)

**Full title:** "Timing Is Everything: Entrainment and Performance in Organization
Theory."
**Authors:** Deborah G. Ancona (MIT Sloan) and Chee-Leong Chong (MIT).
**Source:** *Academy of Management Best Papers Proceedings*, pp. 166–169 (the
uploaded file is `ancona1992.pdf`, a 4-page conference-proceedings paper). Note:
Paper 2 cites the later journal version as "Ancona and Chong 1996"; only the 1992
proceedings text was read here.

### 1. DEFINITION

This paper gives **two explicit definitions** — one for the borrowed biological
concept, and one the authors coin for organization theory.

- **Biological concept** (p. 166, section "The concept of entrainment in biology"):
  > "Entrainment is a process where a biological or behavioral rhythm is modified
  > by external cycles or pacers. However only the periodicities and phases of
  > these rhythms are modified not their occurrences. The modified rhythm persists
  > even after the external pacers are removed (McGrath, Kelly and Machatka, 1986)."

- **The authors' own definition for organization theory** (p. 166, section
  "Entrainment in organization theory"):
  > "We define entrainment as the adjustment or moderation of one behavior to
  > synchronize or to be in rhythm with another."

- A brief working characterization also appears in the Introduction (p. 166):
  > "Entrainment is a concept that explains the alignment of rhythms within and
  > between systems."

The paper is therefore explicit and self-conscious about defining the term; it
does not use it undefined.

### 2. ORIGIN

The paper states plainly that the concept is **borrowed from biology**. The
abstract (p. 166) opens:
> "This paper applies the biological concept of entrainment to organization
> theory."

The cited sources for the biological/behavioral lineage are:
- **McGrath, Kelly, and Machatka, 1986** — cited as the source of the biological
  definition and of the "social psychology of time" work (full reference on p. 169:
  "McGrath, J.E., Kelly, J.R., & Machatka, D.E. 1986. The social psychology of
  time: Entrainment of behavior in social and organizational settings. *Applied
  Social Psychology Annual* 5: 21-44.").
- **Aschoff, 1979** — cited for circadian entrainment and the concept of
  *zeitgebers* (p. 166: "periodic factors in the environment called zeitgebers
  (Aschoff, 1979)").

The paper does **not** trace the term to physics or to Huygens; its stated origin
is biology (chronobiology / circadian rhythms).

### 3. POSITION

Ancona and Chong argue that entrainment should be imported into organization
theory as "a new theoretical lens" (abstract, p. 166) to bring *time* into a field
that has largely ignored it. Their distinctive move is to extend the deterministic
biological notion to allow human agency: "we suggest that in the social sciences
the concept of entrainment extends beyond this determinism to incorporate some
degree of voluntarism" (p. 166) — a system can be entrained, entrain another, or
try to shift the pace of its environment. They build a **typology of three types**
— *tempo*, *synchronic*, and *harmonic* entrainment (p. 166) — illustrate each with
organizational examples across individual, group, and organizational levels, and
propose hypotheses (internal vs. external entrainment; initial vs. subsequent
entrainment) linking entrainment to performance, arguing that entrainment to "the
appropriate zeitgeber is associated with higher performance for a system" (p. 168).

### 4. EVIDENCE LOCATION

- **p. 166 (Abstract & Introduction):** first use, framing as a biological concept
  and "new theoretical lens"; working characterization ("alignment of rhythms
  within and between systems").
- **p. 166 (section "ENTRAINMENT" → "The concept of entrainment in biology"):**
  biological definition; zeitgebers; circadian/free-running examples; jet-lag /
  re-entrainment.
- **p. 166 (section "Entrainment in organization theory"):** the authors' own
  definition; the determinism-vs-voluntarism extension.
- **p. 166–167 (section "TYPES OF ENTRAINMENT..."):** tempo, synchronic, harmonic
  entrainment defined and exemplified.
- **p. 167–168 (Synchronic and cross-level examples):** punctuated equilibrium,
  CEO/top-team succession as entrainment.
- **p. 168 (Harmonic entrainment example):** mentor–mentee complementarity.
- **p. 168 (section "HYPOTHESES"):** internal/external and initial/subsequent
  entrainment; entrainment–performance claims.
- **p. 168–169 (Conclusion):** entrainment as bringing time into organization
  theory; re-entrainment difficulty.

### Related terms / spellings noted (Ancona & Chong)

Uses **"tempo entrainment," "synchronic entrainment," "harmonic entrainment"**
(its three-type typology); also **"internal / external entrainment," "initial /
subsequent entrainment," "mutual entrainment," "re-entrainment."** Related vocabulary:
**"pacer," "zeitgeber," "rhythm," "tempo," "pace," "period," "phase."** Note the term
**"synchronic"** (pace + rhythmic phase) — this is the label this paper uses for what
Paper 2 calls "phase" entrainment.

## Paper 2 — Pérez-Nordtvedt, Payne, Short & Kedia (2008)

**Full title:** "An Entrainment-Based Model of Temporal Organizational Fit, Misfit,
and Performance."
**Authors:** Liliana Pérez-Nordtvedt, G. Tyge Payne, Jeremy C. Short, Ben L. Kedia.
**Source:** *Organization Science* 19(5), September–October 2008, pp. 785–801.

### 1. DEFINITION

This paper defines the term explicitly and repeatedly, distinguishing a general
("traditional") definition from its own organizational-level definition.

- **General / traditional definition** (abstract p. 785, restated in the
  Introduction p. 785):
  > "Entrainment is traditionally defined as the synchronization of the tempo
  > and/or phase of two or more activities within a system (Ancona and Chong 1996,
  > Aschoff 1979, McGrath et al. 1984, Standifer and Bluedorn 2006)."

  The abstract phrases it identically: "Entrainment refers to the synchronization
  of the tempo and/or phase of two or more activities within a system" (p. 785).

- **Social entrainment** — quoted from a cited source (p. 786):
  > "They define social entrainment as the 'capturing and modification of human
  > activity cycles by various social customs, norms, and institutions' (McGrath
  > and Rotchford 1983, p. 78)."

- **The authors' own "organizational entrainment" definition** (p. 788):
  > "organizational entrainment is defined here as the processes by which
  > organizations cope with temporal change by synchronizing (i.e., tempo matching
  > and/or phase aligning) their endogenous cyclic activities to those of the
  > external environment (i.e., the zeitgeber)."

The paper is fully explicit about definitions — it does not use the term undefined.

### 2. ORIGIN

The paper gives a **multi-stage origin story**, more detailed than Paper 1:

- **Physics first, then biology** (p. 785):
  > "The concept of entrainment, first developed in physics and then widely used
  > in biology, holds the potential for organizational scholars to continue this
  > borrowing process and enrich the field of organization science (Ancona and
  > Chong 1996, Bluedorn 1993)."

- **Named originator in physics — Huygens** (p. 786):
  > "The phenomenon of synchronization or 'entrainment of frequency' was first
  > noted by Christian Huygens, a Dutch physicist of the 17th century."
  (The paper recounts his coupled-pendulum-clock observation.)

- **Introduction into the social sciences** (p. 786):
  > "Entrainment was introduced into the social sciences by Hall (1983) and
  > McGrath and Rotchford (1983)."
  With the *social entrainment* concept developed further by McGrath et al. (1984),
  and the *zeitgeber* / circadian lineage attributed to Aschoff (1979) and
  Pittendrigh (1972).

So the stated lineage is: **physics (Huygens, 17th c.) → biology (Aschoff,
Pittendrigh) → social sciences (Hall 1983; McGrath & Rotchford 1983; McGrath et al.
1984) → organization science (Ancona & Chong 1996; Bluedorn 1993)**.

### 3. POSITION

The paper's stance is to move entrainment from metaphor to an explicit,
organization-level theoretical model. It argues that prior work only "mentioned
entrainment's theoretical potential at the organization level, rather than
outlining a specific research agenda" (p. 787), and it fills that gap by building
a conceptual model and eight propositions linking **organization–environment (O-E)
temporal fit/misfit to performance and survival**. Its central claim is that
temporal misfit (in tempo and/or phase) causes inefficiency, substandard
performance, and potential organizational death, and that organizations respond
either by **entraining** (synchronizing to the environmental zeitgeber) or by
**temporal enactment** (imposing their own cycles so the environment entrains to
them) — with a "tolerance range" meaning fit is a matter of degree, not a
dichotomy. Entrainment is positioned as complementary to contingency,
institutional, and population-ecology theories.

### 4. EVIDENCE LOCATION

- **p. 785 (Abstract & Introduction):** traditional definition; tempo/phase
  distinction; physics→biology origin; gaming-console and misfit examples.
- **p. 786 (section "Entrainment in the Social Sciences"):** Huygens origin;
  physics/biology/chemistry/economics/physiology/psychology arenas; Hall (1983)
  and McGrath & Rotchford (1983); social-entrainment definition; zeitgeber;
  McGrath et al. (1984) assumptions.
- **p. 786–787 (Ancona & Chong 1996 discussion; Figure 1):** cycles, tempo, phase,
  magnitude; tempo vs. phase entrainment; tempo misfit → phase misfit.
- **p. 787–788 (section "Organizational Entrainment" → "Key Assumptions"):** five
  assumptions (endogeneity, cycle strength, strategic choice, coping with temporal
  change, cyclical/rhythmic time).
- **p. 788–789 (section "Organizational Entrainment Definition and Types"):** the
  authors' organizational-entrainment definition; entrainment of phase vs. tempo;
  entrainment vs. adaptation; entrainment vs. **pseudoentrainment**.
- **p. 789–794 (the model, Figures 2–3, Propositions 1–5):** O-E temporal fit/misfit
  quadrants; entrainment vs. temporal enactment; tolerance range.
- **p. 794–796 (Propositions 6–8):** difficulty of entrainment; degree/length of
  misfit; **intraentrainment**.
- **p. 796–798 (Discussion & Future Research):** entrainment and contingency /
  institutional / population-ecology theories; "isochronism"; empirical agenda.

### Related terms / spellings noted (Pérez-Nordtvedt et al.)

Uses **"temporal entrainment," "social entrainment," "organizational entrainment,"
"tempo entrainment," "phase entrainment,"** and the coined variants
**"intraentrainment," "extraentrainment,"** and **"pseudoentrainment"** (coincidental
synchronization with no adjustment of tempo/phase). It pairs entrainment with a
contrasting mechanism it calls **"temporal enactment."** Core vocabulary: **"tempo,"
"phase," "cycle," "zeitgeber," "pacer/pacing," "rhythm," "temporal fit/misfit."**
Note this paper labels one type **"phase" entrainment**, where Paper 1 uses the term
**"synchronic."**
