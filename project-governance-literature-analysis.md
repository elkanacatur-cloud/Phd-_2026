## OUTPUT 2 — Narrative Synthesis

### 1. Definitional Landscape — is there consensus on what project governance is?

With nineteen papers the definitional picture is best described as a **stable core, an
explicit acknowledgement of non-consensus, and four framing postures**. The single most
useful addition here is **Ahola et al. (2014)**, whose citation analysis states the
problem plainly — there is "a lack of a shared and universally accepted view of what
project governance actually is" (p. 1324) — and then resolves it into two streams:
governance *external to* a specific project (a parent firm imposing standards on its
projects; principal-agent) and governance *internal to* a specific project (tailored,
shared rules negotiated among the multiple firms delivering one project; grounded in
transaction cost economics). This external/internal cut organises much of the rest of
the corpus: Müller (2009), Biesenthal & Wilden (2014) and Brunet (2021) largely occupy
the *external* stream (governance as the parent/institutional framework over projects),
while Sainati et al. (2020), Denicol (2020) and Qiu et al. (2019) sit in the *internal*
stream (governance as the coordination architecture inside one megaproject).

The *structural mainstream* remains the quotable centre: governance as structures,
processes, roles and accountabilities aligning a project with strategy (Biesenthal &
Wilden 2014, p. 1300, canonising Turner 2009, PMI 2013, Müller 2009; restated by Sainati
p. 244, Qiu p. 427, and Brunet via Klakegg et al. 2008, p. 406). Turner (2020) *layers*
this by separating governance (structure) from **governmentality** (agency) — pivotal
for the RQ, because an election changes governmentality while the structure persists.
Sanderson (2012) *dynamises* it by separating **governance** (ex-ante made order) from
**governing** (ex-post spontaneous order). And a growing set *declines* a
project-governance definition to substitute an alternative object: institutional logics
(Biesenthal et al. 2018), distributive allocation (Livert 2019), political ideology and
symbolism (Menga 2018), polycentric coordination (Araral & Hartley 2013), narrative and
time (Winch & Sergeeva 2022; Ika et al. 2025), coupling (Malherbe 2022), boundary work
(Beck et al. 2024), and megaprojects-as-wicked-policy-fields (Esposito & Terlizzi 2023).
The operative meaning now spans a control architecture, a structure-plus-agency system,
an ex-ante/ex-post process, a distributive-political instrument, a polycentric
coordination order, and a temporally-constituted phenomenon — and the electoral-vs-
project problem lives exactly where these readings overlap.

### 2. Governance Elements Mapping — what components consistently appear?

The four-element spine — **structures/roles/accountabilities**, **decision-making
processes**, **multi-level / cross-organizational structure**, **alignment/control
mechanisms** — is now corroborated across all nineteen and enriched. Müller (2009) adds
capability-maturity (education/PMOs/sponsors/audits); Turner (2020) adds the behavioural
layer (six psychological constructs) and the OECD quartet; Brunet (2021) adds the
efficiency–legitimacy–accountability triangle; Sanderson (2012) adds the governance-
mechanism catalogue and the made-vs-spontaneous distinction; **Denicol (2020)** adds the
role taxonomy (owner/sponsor/client/delivery-partner/operator) and the "multilayer and
multilevel organizational structure" of the megaproject client, plus the client-as-
supply-chain-architect; **Araral & Hartley (2013)** add the polycentric primitives
(bounded autonomy, nested jurisdictions, multi-functional units); **Esposito & Terlizzi
(2023)** add the six-fold complexity typology (technical, political-administrative,
social-cultural, financial, legal-regulatory, organizational); Ika et al. (2025) add the
temporal components (timing norms, orientations, horizons); and Beck et al. (2024) add
boundary objects and the four boundary types.

The **multi-level / cross-organizational** element is now the most densely covered and
most RQ-relevant: Ahola's inter-firm nexus, Qiu's four-government HZMB, Denicol's
multilayer client, Esposito & Terlizzi's "supranational, national, regional, and
municipal" complexity, and Araral & Hartley's explicit polycentric multi-jurisdiction
model all describe governance stretched across levels and organizations — the substrate
on which electoral misalignment would act. The divergence remains theoretical, and two
papers again flag the field's over-reliance on agency/stakeholder theory (Qiu p. 427;
Ika et al.), inviting the institutional and temporal lenses the thesis will use.

### 3. Stakeholder & Decision-Making Patterns — how are actors positioned, and where are the tensions?

All nineteen converge on a **multi-actor, conflict-laden** picture, now spanning
*contained → contested → politically-mediated → symbolically-constructed*. Contained:
Sainati's SPV engineers conflict out ex-ante; Müller's and Ahola's agency problem
between sponsor/PBF and project manager. Mid-spectrum: Turner's multiple simultaneous
governmentalities; Sanderson's three resolution logics; Denicol's role interfaces that
must be set early or fail (Channel Tunnel); Beck et al.'s 120-agency coordination by
deference to expertise; Araral & Hartley's polycentric "partisan mutual adjustment" with
no hegemonic centre. Contested: Biesenthal et al. and Qiu et al. treat conflict as
constitutive (institutional entrepreneurship; system leaders as "conflicting buffer,"
p. 435). Esposito & Terlizzi (2023) formalise the *external* dimension — conflict among
governments, citizens and social movements that can "stop, suspend, delay, or amend
megaproject development" (p. 138).

At the *political-symbolic* end sit the Cluster-D papers. Livert et al. (2019) show
central government disbursing for "its own re-election chances" (p. 7). **Menga (2018)**
positions political elites as the decisive actors who legitimate megaprojects through
discourse, and shows the *deciders themselves changing with elections*: the Strait of
Messina Bridge is assigned, cancelled, revived and cancelled again across the Berlusconi,
Prodi and Monti governments, while the Rogun Dam advances under a single unbroken
presidency (p. 8). **Delcayre & Bourdin (2025)** recast local officials as political
mediators who "start distancing themselves from energy projects" as an election nears
(p. 5). The pattern for the RQ is now decisive: **who decides is never neutral; deciders
change mid-project (Turner's Amsterdam, Denicol's evolving sponsor/client, Brunet's
years-long actor turnover); and in Cluster D the deciders change specifically because of
elections or regime continuity.** Menga and Delcayre & Bourdin are the two papers that
join "deciders change" to "deciders change *because of the electoral calendar*" — one at
national-symbolic scale, one at local-official scale — bracketing exactly the megaproject
governance space the thesis occupies.

### 4. Temporality in Governance — what the papers SAY, and what is MISSING

The enlarged corpus makes this the richest section and, crucially, now contains *two
empirical demonstrations* of electoral-timeline effects (Menga; Delcayre & Bourdin)
plus an explicit *definitional debate* about governance temporality (Ahola et al.).

**Dedicated temporality theory.** Ika, Söderlund & Pinto (2025) is the engine — three
time streams and, decisively, temporal misfit and entrainment ("temporal fit between
project activities and temporal demands imposed by the external environment," pp. 5–6,
with events including "politics, government regulations"), plus divergent manager-vs-
funder horizons (p. 2) and the "paradox of intertemporal choice" (p. 4). Winch & Sergeeva
(2022) add the narrative apparatus (shaping→delivery re-narration across the life-cycle).

**The temporality *of governance itself*.** Ahola et al. (2014) uniquely surface the
question the RQ turns on: is governance a "continuous managerial decision-making process"
or a "relatively static set of mechanisms" (pp. 1323–1324)? If governance is continuous,
an electoral turnover perturbs it in real time; if static, the turnover collides with a
fixed structure. Sanderson's governing-vs-governance is the same insight operationalised,
and Denicol (2020) shows the *concrete* version — governance roles that are "nonstatic and
evolutionary… throughout the project life cycle."

**Governance → time-horizon compression.** Müller (2009) shows economic pressure
"reduces the time horizon view of the project manager drastically"; Sanderson (2012)
shows the political version — promoters "often not in office when the actual viability of
a project can be assessed… significant political capital in the short-term" (p. 436).

**Project-internal temporality.** Qiu et al. (evolutionary complexity), Turner (stage-
varying governmentality), Denicol (life-cycle role evolution), Malherbe (pace-mismatch
over three sequences), Brunet (framework evolution over years), Esposito & Terlizzi
(dynamic complexity; linear vs complex policy time), Beck et al. (temporal boundaries).
Biesenthal & Wilden (2014) remain the flag for *implicit* temporality only.

**Electoral/external temporality.** Five papers now reach outside the project for a
clock. Biesenthal et al. (2018) names the duration-vs-political-cycle tension (p. 45);
Livert (2019) specifies the electoral clock without a project timeline; **Menga (2018)**
demonstrates that *electoral alternation vs authoritarian continuity determines whether a
megaproject advances or stalls* (p. 8); and **Delcayre & Bourdin (2025)** states the
mechanism in plain terms — "electoral mandates are short-lived. This temporal
misalignment reinforces both NIMEY and NIMTOO behaviours" (p. 6).

**What is still MISSING.** Two papers must be flagged for *not* treating temporality as
rhythm/misalignment: Sainati et al. (2020, SPV liquidation only) and Biesenthal & Wilden
(2014, internal horizons only). And despite Menga and Delcayre & Bourdin, **no paper
closes the full loop the RQ requires.** Ika and Winch & Sergeeva give the mechanism but
never instantiate the electoral cycle; the project-internal papers evolve time
endogenously; Livert has the electoral clock but no project timeline; Menga demonstrates
electoral effects on megaprojects but through *ideology/symbolism*, not governance
mechanics; Delcayre & Bourdin demonstrates the temporal-misalignment mechanism but for
*local officials and renewable-energy projects*. The specific, scaled construct — an
exogenous electoral cycle repeatedly turning over the deciders and governmentality across
one long megaproject's timeline, producing recurrent temporal misfit and forced
re-narration — remains unbuilt.

### 5. Megaproject Context — coverage of complex, multi-level projects

Coverage is now extensive and layered. Seven papers are explicitly megaproject-centred —
Sanderson (2012), Menga (2018), Biesenthal et al. (2018), Qiu et al. (2019), Denicol
(2020), Sainati et al. (2020), Brunet (2021) and Esposito & Terlizzi (2023) — spanning
governance critique (Sanderson), political ideology (Menga), institutional theory
(Biesenthal et al.), institutional complexity (Qiu), delivery-model/supply-chain
architecture (Denicol), financial-legal instruments (Sainati), government policy
frameworks (Brunet) and wicked-policy-field theory (Esposito & Terlizzi). Menga is
especially valuable for the RQ because its two cases are chosen precisely to vary the
*political-regime/electoral* dimension; Esposito & Terlizzi because it re-frames
megaproject governance as a *nonlinear policy process* open to political-commitment
change; Denicol because it shows the *government sponsor* as one evolving node in a
multilayer client structure.

The remaining papers cover *inter-organizational and multi-level* criteria without being
megaproject-specific: Müller (2009), Ahola et al. (2014) and Biesenthal & Wilden (2014)
on multi-firm/multi-level governance; Turner (2020) on mixed cases; Araral & Hartley
(2013) on polycentric multi-jurisdiction environmental governance; Winch & Sergeeva
(2022) on the Eden project; Malherbe (2022) on an innovation ecosystem; Beck et al. (2024)
on a 120-agency response; Ika et al. (2025) on temporary organizations generally; and
Livert (2019) and Delcayre & Bourdin (2025) on metropolitan grant allocation and
multi-level energy governance. So the megaproject *context* is very well covered (seven
dedicated papers), the *multi-level coordination* concept is now strongly covered (Ahola,
Qiu, Denicol, Araral & Hartley, Esposito & Terlizzi), and the electoral *mechanism* is
covered (Menga, Livert, Delcayre & Bourdin) — but no single paper covers the megaproject
context, the electoral mechanism, and the temporal machinery at once.

### 6. Research Gap Analysis — what temporal-misalignment research fills

Reading all nineteen together produces the sharpest gap statement of the series. Two
papers (Menga; Delcayre & Bourdin) now *demonstrate* electoral-timeline effects, which
moves the gap from "unexamined" to "demonstrated at the wrong scale / through the wrong
lens":

1. **The mechanism is empirically real at two scales — but not at megaproject-governance
   scale.** Delcayre & Bourdin (2025) prove it for local officials (NIMEY/NIMTOO,
   "temporal misalignment," p. 6); Menga (2018) proves it for national megaproject *fate*
   under electoral alternation vs continuity (p. 8). Neither models the *internal
   governance mechanics* of a multi-year megaproject crossing several electoral cycles —
   the thesis's target.

2. **The horizon-conflict is named corpus-wide but never modelled dynamically.**
   Biesenthal et al. (2018, p. 45) and Sanderson (2012, p. 436) flag duration > political
   tenure; Müller (2009) and Ika et al. (2025) supply the horizon-compression and
   temporal-misfit mechanisms; Ahola et al. (2014) supply the static-vs-continuous
   governance question. Assembling these into a model of *recurring* electoral
   perturbation is open work.

3. **The two clocks are studied apart.** Livert (2019), Menga (2018) and Delcayre &
   Bourdin (2025) own the electoral clock; Qiu, Turner, Brunet, Denicol, Sanderson, Winch
   & Sergeeva and Esposito & Terlizzi own the project clock. Integrating them — importing
   NIMEY / electoral-alternation / core-swing / budget-cycle logic into megaproject
   governance — is the contribution space.

4. **A ready-made temporal apparatus is pointed at elections only in Cluster D.** Ika et
   al. (entrainment/misfit) and Winch & Sergeeva (re-narration) supply the machinery; the
   thesis can model the electoral cycle as an external *zeitgeber* the megaproject fails to
   entrain to, each turnover forcing a re-narration and a governmentality shift.

5. **Governance is modelled as static, endogenously-evolving, or singly-governed — the
   ex-post/governing and polycentric views are the exceptions.** Sainati (static),
   Biesenthal & Wilden (fixed horizons) at one end; Sanderson (governing), Turner
   (Amsterdam restructuring), Denicol (evolving roles) and Araral & Hartley (polycentric
   dynamic order) show governance *changing*. None introduces a *recurring external clock*
   that changes the principals on a fixed cadence — which is what elections do.

6. **The governmentality lens localises where an election bites.** Turner's (2020)
   structure/governmentality split — reinforced by Sanderson's ex-ante/ex-post and
   Denicol's sponsor/client separation — shows that an election changes the governmentality
   (and often the government *sponsor*) while the durable governance structure and
   contracts (Sainati's SPVs) persist. The friction between a *durable* structure and a
   *cyclically-changing* governmentality/sponsor is a crisp, novel formulation.

7. **Existing conflict/complexity typologies lack a temporal-electoral category — except
   NIMEY.** Qiu's six complexity types, Esposito & Terlizzi's six, and Biesenthal et al.'s
   three pillars have no electoral-timing conflict type; Delcayre & Bourdin's NIMEY/NIMTOO
   is the one template that does, built for local politics. The study can adapt and scale
   it — potentially as a seventh "electoral-temporal" complexity type for megaprojects.

**Bottom line for the transfer report.** The nineteen papers supply every component the
research question needs: a stable structural definition plus Ahola's external/internal
streams, Turner's structure/governmentality split, and Sanderson's governance/governing
split; an institutional-logics, complexity and wicked-policy-field frame (Biesenthal et
al.; Qiu; Esposito & Terlizzi); seven megaproject cases including government policy
frameworks (Brunet), delivery-model architecture (Denicol) and an explicitly
electoral-comparative pair (Menga); a dedicated temporal apparatus (Ika; Winch &
Sergeeva) plus governance→horizon-compression mechanisms (Müller; Sanderson); rich
multi-level and inter-organizational coordination models (Ahola; Qiu; Denicol; Araral &
Hartley; Beck et al.; Malherbe); and, in Cluster D, both quantitative electoral-allocation
evidence (Livert) and two direct empirical demonstrations of electoral-timeline effects
(Menga at national/symbolic scale; Delcayre & Bourdin at local-official scale via
NIMEY/NIMTOO). What none of them does — and what the thesis is positioned to deliver — is
the **integration at megaproject-governance scale**: a governance account in which an
exogenous electoral cycle, turning over the deciders, the government sponsor, and their
governmentality repeatedly across a megaproject's multi-year, multi-electoral-term
timeline, becomes a first-class driver of governance outcomes through recurrent temporal
misfit, forced re-narration, and friction between durable structure and cyclical
governmentality. Menga and Delcayre & Bourdin prove the mechanism is real; every other
paper supplies a piece of the frame; none assembles them for megaproject governance.
That assembly is the study's contribution.
