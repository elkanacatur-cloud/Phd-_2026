# Project Governance, Temporality & Megaprojects — Systematic Literature Analysis

> Prepared for the PhD transfer report. Research question anchoring the reading:
> **How does misalignment between electoral cycles and megaproject timelines shape
> governance outcomes?**
>
> Five papers analysed (chronological):
> 1. **Biesenthal & Wilden (2014)** — "Multi-level project governance: Trends and
>    opportunities," *International Journal of Project Management* 32, pp. 1291–1308.
> 2. **Biesenthal, Clegg, Mahalingam & Sankaran (2018)** — "Applying institutional
>    theories to managing megaprojects," *IJPM* 36, pp. 43–54.
> 3. **Livert, Gainza & Acuña (2019)** — "Paving the electoral way: Urban
>    infrastructure, partisan politics and civic engagement," *World Development*
>    124, art. 104628, pp. 1–14.
> 4. **Qiu, Chen, Sheng & Cheng (2019)** — "Governance of institutional complexity
>    in megaproject organizations," *IJPM* 37, pp. 425–443.
> 5. **Sainati, Locatelli, Smith, Brookes & Olver (2020)** — "Types and functions of
>    special purpose vehicles in infrastructure megaprojects," *IJPM* 38, pp. 243–255.
>
> Every quotation carries a page number. Where a paper does **not** address a
> requested dimension (especially temporality), this is flagged explicitly rather
> than glossed.

## OUTPUT 2 — Narrative Synthesis

### 1. Definitional Landscape — is there consensus on what project governance is?

There is **partial consensus with a persistent centre of gravity, but no settled
definition**, and the corpus splits along a disciplinary fault line. The clearest
statement of the mainstream comes through Biesenthal & Wilden (2014), whose
Leximancer review distils the field to a recurring triad: project governance "is to
propose a framework that combines processes, roles and accountabilities aimed at
delivering projects" (p. 1300), operating to "align project objectives with the
strategy of the larger organization" (p. 1305). Their Table 2 (p. 1294) canonises
the three most-cited definitions — Turner (2009, p. 311), PMI (2013, p. 579) and
Müller (2009, p. 4) — all of which share the "structure–process–objectives–
monitoring" spine. Sainati et al. (2020, p. 244) adopt this directly ("Project
governance defines both the organisational structures and processes to govern the
project… assigning project roles and responsibilities"), and Qiu et al. (2019,
p. 427) echo it functionally: governance regulates "the roles, practices,
accountabilities, and decision-making… to achieve a centrally coordinated project
planning and control function."

Against this structural mainstream stand two productive dissenters. Biesenthal
et al. (2018) decline to define project governance at all, folding it into an
**institutional** account where governance is one facet of "creating, organizing and
managing a complex institutional field" (p. 47) — a deliberate move away from
structure-and-process toward legitimacy and contested logics. Livert et al. (2019)
belong to a different literature entirely — *distributive politics* and *urban/
metropolitan governance* — and never use "project governance" as a term of art; for
them governance is the political-economic machinery allocating public infrastructure
across a fragmented metropolitan space (p. 1). The upshot for the transfer report:
the *nominal* definition is stable and quotable, but the *operative* meaning ranges
from a control architecture (Biesenthal & Wilden; Sainati) to a field of institutional
struggle (Biesenthal et al.; Qiu) to a distributive political instrument (Livert).
That range is itself an argument for the thesis — electoral-vs-project misalignment
lives precisely where the "control architecture" and the "political instrument"
readings collide.

### 2. Governance Elements Mapping — what components consistently appear?

Four elements recur across the corpus, and their consistency is high enough to treat
as a stable spine:

- **Structures, roles and accountabilities.** Universal. Biesenthal & Wilden's
  central finding is the "structure/process/role" cluster (p. 1300); Qiu et al. name
  "roles, practices, accountabilities" (p. 427); Sainati et al. operationalise roles
  through SPV types and contractual positions.
- **Decision-making processes.** Universal, though the locus shifts: the PMO/board
  (Biesenthal & Wilden), the SPV/Project Company (Sainati et al., p. 248), the
  system-leader hierarchy (Qiu et al., p. 436), the central disburser plus mayors
  (Livert et al.), or the institutional entrepreneur (Biesenthal et al.).
- **Multi-level / cross-organizational structure.** Strong. Biesenthal & Wilden's
  headline is the "multi-level phenomenon" spanning project/PMO/organization
  (p. 1304); Qiu et al. and Sainati et al. add the cross-jurisdiction layer; Livert
  et al. supply the central–regional–local tier logic (p. 4).
- **Alignment / control mechanisms.** Strong. Whether contractual (Sainati's SPVs,
  Biesenthal & Wilden's agency-theory contracts) or relational (stewardship, trust)
  or institutional (Qiu's localization, system leaders), every paper posits a
  mechanism that binds dispersed actors to a common objective.

The **divergence** is theoretical rather than componential. Biesenthal & Wilden map
five governance theories onto levels (agency/TCE/RDT low, stewardship/stakeholder
high; Table 4, p. 1304); Sainati et al. run on Transaction Cost Theory
("organizations adapt their governance structures to achieve the lowest possible
transaction costs," p. 244); Qiu et al. and Biesenthal et al. displace all of these
with **institutional logics**, arguing governance research "predominantly examined
governance from the perspectives of agency theory and stakeholder theory… without
considering the significance of institutional logics" (Qiu et al., p. 427). For a
thesis on electoral misalignment, the institutional-logics wing is the more fertile
substrate, because it already contains the vocabulary (competing logics, constraint
conflicts) for time-based clashes.

### 3. Stakeholder & Decision-Making Patterns — how are actors positioned, and where are the tensions?

Every paper converges on a **multi-actor, conflict-laden** picture, and they arrange
the actors on a spectrum from *contained* to *contested*. At the contained end,
Sainati et al. (2020) treat stakeholders as holders of a "legal, or contractual,
claim" (p. 244) whose conflicts are pre-empted structurally: the SPV "clusters
multiple stakeholders under a common incorporated vehicle" (p. 247) and lenders
"retain significant controlling power" (p. 248). Conflict is engineered out ex-ante
through ring-fencing and risk channelling. Biesenthal & Wilden (2014) sit slightly
further along: conflict is real but theory-resolvable — agency theory answers it with
monitoring and incentives, stewardship with trust, stakeholder theory with
"balancing the interests and claims of different stakeholders" (p. 1300).

At the contested end, Biesenthal et al. (2018) and Qiu et al. (2019) treat conflict as
constitutive, not residual. Biesenthal et al. describe megaprojects as arenas of
"differently organized subjectivities: politicians as sponsors; bankers as creditors;
communities as resistance" (p. 51), where "polycentric governance structures obfuscate
hierarchical decision making" (p. 49) and cognitive conflicts are "the most difficult
to resolve" (p. 47). Qiu et al. make the resolution machinery explicit: "system
leaders acted as a conflicting buffer" (p. 435), backed by a five-level task hierarchy
and four-level organization hierarchy (p. 436). Livert et al. (2019) supply the most
sharply *political* positioning: the central government decides disbursement to serve
its "own re-election chances" (p. 7), while "long-lasting local mayors campaign for
grant allocation" (p. 7) — decision-making is openly a contest for electoral credit,
and the proposed resolution is institutional redesign (an elected two-tier metropolitan
authority as "checks and balances," p. 10).

The pattern that matters for the RQ: **who decides is never neutral, and in the two
politically-attuned papers (Livert; Biesenthal et al.) the deciders are themselves
subject to an electoral clock.** That is the seam the thesis can open — most PM papers
positioned stakeholders by role or contract; only the political papers positioned them
by *electoral time*.

### 4. Temporality in Governance — what the papers SAY, and what is MISSING

This is where the corpus is thinnest and most uneven, and the gaps must be stated
plainly.

**What is said.** Only Biesenthal et al. (2018) name the exact tension at the heart of
the RQ: megaproject "Duration: Time spans often extending beyond the political life
cycle of a national government" (p. 45), and institutional orders "whose temporality
may not last beyond the duration of the project" (p. 49). Their "reverse clocks"
example (p. 50) even dramatises time as a governance instrument. Qiu et al. (2019)
supply the richest *internal* temporality: "evolutionary complexity, because
megaprojects are dynamic during their entire life cycle" (p. 425), system leaders that
"evolve during the different stages of the project's life cycle" (p. 437), and the
explicit finding that "responses to institutional complexity are related to time"
(p. 437); they also import Dille & Söderlund's temporal fit/misfit and Dille et al.'s
temporal avoidance/splitting/matching (pp. 427–428). Livert et al. (2019) provide the
opposite face — a fully specified **electoral** temporality (political budget cycles,
transfers rising "as the electoral race approaches," p. 2; four-year fixed calendars;
tenure/"Periods" effects, p. 7) — but no *project* time at all.

**What is missing.** Biesenthal & Wilden (2014) reduce time to an internal level
attribute: "short-term (temporary)" at project level vs "long-term (permanent)" at
organizational level (Table 4, p. 1304), with no external clock and no notion of
cyclicity — an explicit flag that this foundational review does **not** engage
temporality as rhythm or misalignment. Sainati et al. (2020) barely register time at
all: the SPV is merely "liquidated when [its] functions have been accomplished"
(p. 252) — **this paper does not treat temporality** and must be flagged as such. And
crucially, **no paper in the set closes the loop the RQ requires**: the two papers with
project time (Qiu; Biesenthal et al.) treat time as *endogenous* life-cycle evolution,
while the one paper with electoral time (Livert) treats infrastructure as fungible
spending with no timeline. The specific mechanism — an *exogenous electoral cycle*
turning over the deciders *repeatedly across a single long project's timeline* — is
named once (Biesenthal et al., p. 45) and modelled nowhere. That un-modelled seam is
the thesis's opening.

### 5. Megaproject Context — coverage of complex, multi-level projects

Coverage is strong but concentrated in the later papers. Three of the five —
Biesenthal et al. (2018), Qiu et al. (2019), Sainati et al. (2020) — are explicitly
megaproject papers, all adopting the Flyvbjerg/Denicol US$1bn-plus, multi-stakeholder,
long-duration definition (Biesenthal et al., p. 44; Sainati et al., p. 244). Qiu et
al.'s HZMB case (a US$9.2bn cross-border bridge under four governments) is the richest
worked example of exactly the "complex, multi-level, multi-stakeholder" context the RQ
targets, and its "political complexity… because multiple governments' interests and
expectations on the project may differ" (p. 425) sits one short step from electoral
misalignment. Sainati et al. add the financial-legal architecture (PPP/PF, SPVs) that
real megaprojects are built on, and Biesenthal et al. supply the theoretical lens
(institutional fields, entrepreneurs) for reading their politics.

The two earlier/other papers cover the *inter-organizational and multi-level* criteria
without being megaproject-specific. Biesenthal & Wilden (2014) address multi-level PBOs
and flag megaprojects only as a risk-heavy sub-case needing "sophisticated governance
mechanisms" (p. 1303). Livert et al. (2019) are not about megaprojects at all — their
unit is many small urban grants — but they contribute the one thing the megaproject
papers lack: a rigorous, quantitative account of how *electoral incentives* distort
infrastructure allocation across a *fragmented multi-tier* governance system (p. 4).
For the transfer report, the sensible framing is that the megaproject *context* is
well covered (Qiu, Sainati, Biesenthal et al.) and the electoral *mechanism* is well
covered (Livert), but **no single paper covers both at once** — which is the study's
contribution space.

### 6. Research Gap Analysis — what temporal-misalignment research fills

Reading the five together produces a clean, defensible gap statement built from the
papers' own words rather than asserted from outside:

1. **The horizon-conflict is named but not mechanised.** Biesenthal et al. (2018,
   p. 45) explicitly observe that megaproject duration exceeds "the political life
   cycle of a national government," and their research agenda asks "How do
   institutional dynamics influence outcomes on megaprojects?" (p. 49) — but they stop
   at invitation. A study of electoral-cycle/megaproject-timeline misalignment
   supplies the missing causal mechanism their agenda requests.

2. **The two temporalities are studied apart, never together.** Livert et al. (2019)
   own the *electoral* clock (budget cycles, tenure, alignment) with no project
   timeline; Qiu et al. (2019) and Biesenthal et al. (2018) own the *project* life
   cycle with the political actors treated as concurrent jurisdictions rather than
   electorally-refreshed bodies. The thesis integrates the two clocks — importing
   Livert's core/swing and political-budget-cycle mechanisms into the megaproject
   governance frame of Qiu and Biesenthal et al.

3. **Governance is modelled as static or endogenously-evolving, not electorally
   perturbed.** Sainati et al. (2020) render governance as a fixed legal-financial
   architecture (no time); Biesenthal & Wilden (2014) render it as fixed level-horizons
   (short vs long). Neither allows for an *external* clock that repeatedly changes the
   principals mid-project. Qiu et al. allow internal evolution but not exogenous
   electoral turnover. Temporal-misalignment research fills this by making the
   *deciders themselves* time-variant across the project's life.

4. **Existing conflict typologies lack a temporal-source category.** Qiu et al.'s
   six-fold complexity (regulatory/political/social/cultural/relational/evolutionary,
   p. 436) and Biesenthal et al.'s three pillars have no explicit *electoral-timing*
   conflict type, even though Qiu's "political complexity" and "evolutionary
   complexity" both border it. The study can propose electoral misalignment as a
   distinct temporal source of institutional conflict — extending, not overturning,
   these frameworks.

**Bottom line for the transfer report.** The corpus gives you (a) a stable structural
definition of project governance to work from, (b) an institutional-logics frame that
already accommodates competing-time conflicts, (c) a megaproject context richly
described (HZMB, PPP/PF, institutional fields), and (d) a fully-specified electoral
mechanism from the distributive-politics literature. What it does **not** give you —
and what the research question is precisely positioned to deliver — is the integration:
a governance account in which an exogenous electoral cycle, turning over the deciders
across a megaproject's multi-year (often multi-electoral-term) timeline, becomes a
first-class driver of governance outcomes. Every paper points at this seam; none
crosses it.
