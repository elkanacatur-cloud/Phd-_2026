## OUTPUT 2 — Narrative Synthesis

### 1. Definitional Landscape — is there consensus on what project governance is?

Across twenty-four papers the picture is a **stable core, an explicit consensus on
non-consensus, and a clear historical drift from "control" to "adaptation."** The most
authoritative new statement is Bakhshi et al. (2025), a systematic review of 636 articles
that charts project-governance thinking "from control to adaptation" and consolidates the
canonical definitions: PMI (2021) — governance as "guidance of project activities through
frameworks, processes, and functions aligned with strategic, organizational, and
operational objectives" — and Müller's (2016) tripartite "controls / processes /
relationships." It also endorses Ahola et al.'s (2014) enduring **external/internal**
distinction, which remains the field's best organising cut: governance *external to* a
project (a parent/institution imposing standards) versus governance *internal to* a
project (coordination among the multiple firms delivering one project). Ahola et al. also
supply the field's honest disclaimer — "there is clearly a lack of a shared and
universally accepted view of what project governance actually is" (p. 1324).

The *structural mainstream* (Biesenthal & Wilden 2014, p. 1300; Sainati p. 244; Qiu
p. 427; Brunet via Klakegg et al. 2008) is now flanked by several dynamic refinements
directly relevant to the RQ. Turner (2020) separates governance (structure) from
**governmentality** (agency). Sanderson (2012) separates **governance** (ex-ante made
order) from **governing** (ex-post spontaneous order). Bourne et al. (2023) reconceive
governance as something that must **move** — "moving goals and moving governance" over the
long life of a megaproject. Unterhitzenberger et al. (2023) add a *vertical* refinement —
a three-tier stack of **metagovernance / governance of networks / network governance** in
which the top layer ("metagovernance") is "typically set by the owner or investor,"
including "public government organizations." And Afieroho et al. (2024) recast governance
as **governmentality/meta-organizing** — the state steering multi-stakeholder arenas.

A further set *declines* a project-governance definition to substitute an alternative
object: institutional logics (Biesenthal et al. 2018), distributive allocation (Livert
2019), political ideology (Menga 2018), polycentric coordination (Araral & Hartley 2013),
narrative and time (Winch & Sergeeva 2022; Ika et al. 2025), coupling (Malherbe 2022),
boundary work (Beck et al. 2024), and wicked-policy-field theory (Esposito & Terlizzi
2023). The operative meaning of "project governance" now spans a control architecture, a
structure-plus-agency system, an ex-ante/ex-post process, a *moving* control system, a
vertical metagovernance stack, a governmentality practice, and a temporally-constituted
phenomenon — and the electoral-vs-project problem lives where these readings overlap.

### 2. Governance Elements Mapping — what components consistently appear?

The four-element spine — **structures/roles/accountabilities**, **decision-making
processes**, **multi-level / cross-organizational structure**, **alignment/control
mechanisms** — holds across all twenty-four and is now richly elaborated. Bourne et al.
(2023) add the **alignment-over-time** element (the "fit" between context, goals and
governance, and the six systemic errors — design, experience, bias, phase change, drift,
intervention — that break it). Unterhitzenberger et al. (2023) add the **vertical
metagovernance stack** and the Type I (hierarchical) / Type II (nonhierarchical) split.
Brunet & Choinière (2024) add **institutional layering and path dependency** as elements
that make governance frameworks thicken over time. Afieroho et al. (2024) add
**meta-organizing and responsibilization** as government coordination instruments.
Bakhshi et al. (2025) add the **theory landscape** itself and name "adaptive/flexible
governance" and "temporal governance" as emerging elements. These sit on top of the
earlier additions (Müller's capability maturity; Turner's psychological constructs and
OECD quartet; Brunet's efficiency–legitimacy–accountability triangle; Denicol's role
taxonomy; Araral & Hartley's polycentric primitives; Esposito & Terlizzi's six-fold
complexity; Ika's timing norms).

The **multi-level / cross-organizational** element is now the densest and most RQ-relevant:
Ahola's inter-firm nexus, Qiu's four-government HZMB, Denicol's multilayer client,
Unterhitzenberger's three-tier stack, Esposito & Terlizzi's "supranational, national,
regional, and municipal" complexity, Araral & Hartley's polycentric jurisdictions, and
Afieroho's federal/state/local tiers all describe governance stretched across levels —
the substrate on which electoral misalignment acts. Two reviews (Qiu p. 427; Bakhshi et
al. 2025) explicitly flag the under-use of institutional, adaptive and *temporal* lenses,
inviting the thesis's contribution.

### 3. Stakeholder & Decision-Making Patterns — how are actors positioned, and where are the tensions?

All twenty-four converge on a **multi-actor, conflict-laden** picture, now spanning
*contained → contested → politically-mediated → state-steered*. Contained: Sainati's SPV,
Müller's and Ahola's sponsor/PM agency problem, Unterhitzenberger's Type I hierarchy with
"clear roles… distinct accountabilities." Mid-spectrum: Turner's multiple governmentalities,
Sanderson's three logics, Denicol's role interfaces, Bourne et al.'s client/contractor
mandate-misalignments (a change of £5m "should be taken a few levels up" on the client
side but decided directly on the contractor side, p. 5), Beck et al.'s 120-agency
coordination by expertise, Araral & Hartley's polycentric mutual adjustment. Contested:
Biesenthal et al. and Qiu treat conflict as constitutive; Esposito & Terlizzi formalise
external conflict (governments, citizens, movements) that can "stop, suspend, delay, or
amend" a megaproject (p. 138).

At the *political / state-steered* end sit the Cluster-D papers plus two new ones.
Livert et al. (2019) show central government disbursing for re-election. Menga (2018)
shows the deciders themselves changing with elections (the Messina Bridge assigned,
cancelled, revived, cancelled across cabinets, p. 8). **Afieroho et al. (2024)** put the
government in the explicit "steering role" as the dominant stakeholder using
governmentality — and document the friction directly: a two-year delay in a megaproject
"because the federal and state governments were controlled by different political parties
at the time" (p. 5), with officials chasing "political mileage for the next election."
**Brunet & Choinière (2024)** show the *governance framework's* actor set expanding and
contracting with each change of government. **Delcayre & Bourdin (2025)** recast local
officials as political mediators timing their positions to the electoral calendar.

The pattern for the RQ is now overwhelming: **who decides is never neutral; the deciders
and their governmentality change mid-project (Turner's Amsterdam, Denicol's evolving
sponsor, Brunet's years-long turnover, Bourne's UK hospital programme imposing new
governance on in-flight projects); and in Clusters C–D the change is explicitly driven by
elections, partisan control, or government alternation.** Menga, Afieroho, Brunet &
Choinière and Delcayre & Bourdin are the four papers that join "deciders change" to
"deciders change *because of politics/elections*" — at national-symbolic, cross-level-
partisan, framework-trajectory, and local-official scales respectively.

### 4. Temporality in Governance — what the papers SAY, and what is MISSING

The enlarged corpus makes this the strongest section and now contains **multiple empirical
demonstrations of political-time effects, a dedicated "moving governance" theory, and a
field-level flag that temporal governance is under-theorised.**

**Dedicated temporality theory.** Ika, Söderlund & Pinto (2025) remains the engine —
temporal misfit and entrainment ("temporal fit between project activities and temporal
demands imposed by the external environment," pp. 5–6, incl. "politics, government
regulations"), divergent manager-vs-funder horizons (p. 2). Winch & Sergeeva (2022) add
the narrative re-projection apparatus.

**Governance that must move over time.** Bourne et al. (2023) is the pivotal new addition:
it theorises that "the governance needs to change as the requirements of the project
change… over the extended length of megaprojects," names "political pressures… over the
life of the megaproject" (p. 8), and supplies six **systemic errors** — of which *drift*
("the project context changes and the governance approach does not adapt"), *phase change*,
and *intervention* ("get a grip" pressure) are ready-made categories for electoral-misfit
failures. Bakhshi et al. (2025) confirm at field level that "temporal governance" and
"sociopolitical changes as critical triggers for governance adaptations" are "often
inadequately addressed."

**The temporality *of governance itself*.** Ahola et al. (2014) surface the continuous-vs-
static governance question; Sanderson's governing-vs-governance operationalises it; Denicol
(2020) and Unterhitzenberger et al. (2023) show governance roles/levels evolving over the
life cycle; Brunet & Choinière (2024) show the *framework* evolving across four decades and
five governments.

**Governance → time-horizon compression.** Müller (2009) and Sanderson (2012, p. 436, the
promoter-out-of-office gap) show pressure and political tenure collapsing the decision
horizon.

**Electoral/external temporality — now five demonstrations.** Biesenthal et al. (2018)
names the duration-vs-political-cycle tension; Livert (2019) specifies the electoral clock
without a project timeline; Menga (2018) shows alternation-vs-continuity determining
megaproject fate; **Afieroho et al. (2024)** shows federal–state *partisan* misalignment
delaying a megaproject two years; **Brunet & Choinière (2024)** shows governance frameworks
re-layering with each change of government; and Delcayre & Bourdin (2025) states the
mechanism outright (NIMEY; "electoral mandates are short-lived. This temporal misalignment
reinforces both NIMEY and NIMTOO behaviours," p. 6).

**What is still MISSING.** Two papers are flagged for *not* treating temporality as
rhythm/misalignment: Sainati et al. (2020, SPV liquidation only) and Biesenthal & Wilden
(2014, internal horizons only). And despite the five demonstrations, **no paper closes the
full loop the RQ requires.** Ika, Winch & Sergeeva and Bourne give the mechanism (misfit,
re-narration, moving governance) but keep the external clock *generic*; Livert has the
electoral clock but no project timeline; Menga works through ideology; Afieroho through
governmentality/community engagement; Brunet & Choinière through institutional layering at
*framework* level rather than *single-project* level; Delcayre & Bourdin at local-official
scale. The specific, scaled construct — an exogenous electoral cycle repeatedly turning
over the deciders, government sponsor and governmentality across *one long megaproject's*
timeline, producing recurrent temporal misfit, governance "drift/intervention," and forced
re-narration — remains unbuilt.

### 5. Megaproject Context — coverage of complex, multi-level projects

Coverage is now extensive: nine explicitly megaproject-centred papers — Sanderson (2012),
Menga (2018), Biesenthal et al. (2018), Qiu et al. (2019), Denicol (2020), Sainati et al.
(2020), Brunet (2021), Bourne et al. (2023), Esposito & Terlizzi (2023) — plus Afieroho
et al. (2024) and Brunet & Choinière (2024) on public–private and public megaprojects, and
Unterhitzenberger et al. (2023) on the megaproject-relevant network layer. Bourne et al.
is especially valuable: its cases (a 24-year Swedish windfarm, a Dutch road programme, a
UK government "national priority" hospital programme) directly exhibit governance moving
over multi-year, politically-exposed timelines. Brunet & Choinière situates megaproject
governance inside four decades of *government policy*. Afieroho shows a PPP megaproject
(Lekki-Epe, 30-year concession) stalled by cross-level partisan conflict.

The remaining papers cover *inter-organizational and multi-level* criteria without being
megaproject-specific (Müller; Ahola; Biesenthal & Wilden; Turner; Araral & Hartley; Winch &
Sergeeva; Malherbe; Beck et al.; Ika; Livert; Delcayre & Bourdin; and the Bakhshi review).
So the megaproject *context* is very well covered, *multi-level coordination* is strongly
covered (Ahola, Qiu, Denicol, Unterhitzenberger, Araral & Hartley, Esposito & Terlizzi,
Afieroho), and the electoral *mechanism* is covered at several scales (Menga, Livert,
Afieroho, Brunet & Choinière, Delcayre & Bourdin) — but no single paper covers the
megaproject context, the electoral mechanism, and the temporal machinery at once.

### 6. Research Gap Analysis — what temporal-misalignment research fills

Reading all twenty-four together yields the sharpest, best-evidenced gap statement of the
series. Political-time effects are now demonstrated at **five scales**, and a field review
(Bakhshi et al. 2025) names the missing agenda, so the gap is "demonstrated in fragments,
never assembled at megaproject-governance scale":

1. **The mechanism is empirically real at multiple scales — never integrated.** Delcayre &
   Bourdin (local officials, NIMEY), Menga (national megaproject fate), Afieroho (cross-level
   partisan delay), and Brunet & Choinière (framework re-layering per government) each show a
   *piece*; none models the internal governance mechanics of one multi-year megaproject
   crossing several electoral cycles.

2. **A "moving governance" theory now exists but its clock is generic.** Bourne et al.
   (2023) supply the mechanism (moving goals/governance; drift, phase change, intervention),
   and Bakhshi et al. (2025) confirm "temporal governance… often inadequately addressed."
   Specialising this to a *recurring electoral* clock is open work.

3. **The two clocks are studied apart.** Livert, Menga, Afieroho, Brunet & Choinière and
   Delcayre & Bourdin own the political/electoral clock; Qiu, Turner, Brunet, Denicol,
   Sanderson, Winch & Sergeeva, Bourne, Unterhitzenberger and Esposito & Terlizzi own the
   project/framework clock. Integrating them is the contribution space.

4. **A ready-made temporal apparatus is pointed at elections only in Cluster D.** Ika
   (entrainment/misfit), Winch & Sergeeva (re-narration) and Bourne (moving governance)
   supply the machinery; the thesis can model the electoral cycle as an external *zeitgeber*
   the megaproject fails to entrain to, each turnover causing governance drift, an
   "intervention" reflex, and forced re-narration.

5. **The vertical governance stack localises where an election bites.** Unterhitzenberger
   et al.'s (2023) **metagovernance** — "set by… public government organizations" — is
   precisely the layer an electoral change perturbs; Turner's governmentality and Denicol's
   government-sponsor node say the same at the agency/role level. The friction between a
   *durable* lower structure (network governance, SPVs, contracts) and a *cyclically-changing
   metagovernance/governmentality* is a crisp, novel formulation.

6. **Governance is modelled as static, endogenously-evolving, layering, or singly-governed —
   the recurring-external-clock case is unbuilt.** Sainati (static) and Biesenthal & Wilden
   (fixed horizons) at one end; Bourne (moving), Brunet & Choinière (layering across
   governments), Turner (restructuring), Denicol (evolving roles), Araral & Hartley
   (polycentric) show governance *changing* — but none imposes a *fixed-cadence external
   clock* that changes the principals repeatedly, which is what elections do.

7. **Existing conflict/complexity/error typologies lack a temporal-electoral category —
   except NIMEY.** Qiu's six complexity types, Esposito & Terlizzi's six, Bourne's six
   systemic errors and Biesenthal et al.'s three pillars have no electoral-timing category;
   Delcayre & Bourdin's NIMEY/NIMTOO is the one template that does, built for local politics.
   The study can adapt and scale it — potentially as an "electoral-temporal" complexity type
   or a seventh systemic error ("electoral drift") for megaprojects.

**Bottom line for the transfer report.** The twenty-four papers supply, between them, every
component the research question needs: a stable structural definition plus Ahola's
external/internal streams, Turner's structure/governmentality split, Sanderson's
governance/governing split, and Bourne's *moving-governance* theory; a vertical
metagovernance stack (Unterhitzenberger) whose top layer is government-set; an
institutional-logics, complexity and wicked-policy-field frame (Biesenthal et al.; Qiu;
Esposito & Terlizzi); a dedicated temporal apparatus (Ika; Winch & Sergeeva) and a
field-level flag that temporal/sociopolitical governance is under-theorised (Bakhshi et
al.); nine-plus megaproject cases including government-policy frameworks (Brunet; Brunet &
Choinière) and long-duration politically-exposed cases (Bourne); and, in Clusters C–D,
*five* empirical demonstrations of political-time effects on projects (Menga; Livert;
Afieroho; Brunet & Choinière; Delcayre & Bourdin). What none of them does — and what the
thesis is positioned to deliver — is the **integration at megaproject-governance scale**: a
governance account in which an exogenous electoral cycle, turning over the deciders, the
government sponsor, the metagovernance layer and their governmentality repeatedly across a
megaproject's multi-year, multi-electoral-term timeline, becomes a first-class driver of
governance outcomes through recurrent temporal misfit, governance drift/intervention,
forced re-narration, and friction between durable structure and cyclical governmentality.
The corpus proves the mechanism is real in fragments and supplies every piece of the frame;
none assembles them for megaproject governance. That assembly is the study's contribution.
