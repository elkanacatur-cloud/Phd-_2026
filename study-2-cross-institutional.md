## 3.4 Research Design and Methods

### 3.4.1 Rationale for Design

This study asks how different institutional contexts influence the misalignment between electoral cycles and megaproject timelines. To answer this question, Study 2 adopts a cross-institutional, semi-structured interview design that generates intensive data from actors positioned across the several institutions whose timing norms bear on major infrastructure delivery. The sensitising concepts developed in Study 1, such as timing norms and temporal misfit, serve as an orienting framework for this design rather than as a fixed set of variables to be measured. They direct attention towards where temporal misalignment is likely to surface and how actors account for it, while leaving room for the data to qualify, extend, or displace them. The paragraphs that follow define the semi-structured interview, justify its fit with the study's critical realist and abductive commitments, and set out why it is preferred to the main alternatives.

A semi-structured interview is a guided conversation in which the researcher works from a prepared set of topics and open questions yet retains the freedom to follow a participant's account wherever it proves analytically useful. It sits between the fixed sequence of a structured questionnaire and the open flow of an unstructured interview. Four features characterise it:

- It follows an interview guide organised around main topics and prompts, not a rigid, pre-coded questionnaire.
- It allows the researcher to pursue themes that arise during the exchange, including ones the guide did not anticipate.
- It balances consistency across informants against responsiveness to each participant's context and vocabulary.
- It treats the researcher as an active instrument whose questioning and interpretation shape the data that result.

These strengths carry corresponding limits. The method depends on what actors can remember and are willing to articulate; it is exposed to retrospective rationalisation, whereby participants reconstruct past decisions as more coherent than they were; and it reaches only those actors who are accessible and willing to take part. For this study these limits are acceptable, because the research question concerns how actors understand and account for temporal misalignment, not a behavioural record independent of their sense-making. Their reasons and interpretations are the very material the analysis requires, and the partiality of any single account becomes evidence about institutional position rather than error to be removed.

The design suits this study for three connected reasons. First, it aligns with the critical realist position set out in Section 3.2. Fletcher (2017) argued that critical realism treats the social world as stratified, with generative mechanisms residing at a level of reality that is not open to direct observation. Such mechanisms become accessible only through the meanings, reasons, and accounts of the actors whose activities sustain them (Bhaskar, 1979; Fletcher, 2017). A semi-structured interview provides precisely this access, generating the intensive accounts from which the study can move, by retroductive inference, towards the institutional mechanisms that produce the observed misalignment between electoral cycles and megaproject timelines. Second, the design fits the study's abductive logic. The sensitising concepts from Study 1 enter the interview as a starting point for enquiry, not as hypotheses to be confirmed or refuted. The flexibility of the format lets the researcher test these concepts against each account and refine the framework as the data accumulate, sustaining the iteration between prior conceptualisation and emerging evidence that abduction requires. Third, the design supports cross-institutional comparison. A shared interview guide holds the main lines of questioning steady across institutions, which makes comparison possible, while the latitude to depart from the guide allows the researcher to follow the contextual variations specific to each setting. This matters because actors drawn from different institutions bring different vocabularies and different concerns to the same phenomenon, and the method accommodates that variation without forcing it into a single frame.

Three alternative designs merit comparison, since each is plausible yet falls short for this study. Ethnography would offer sustained, first-hand observation, but it depends on prolonged access that senior institutional actors rarely grant, and the timeframe of this study does not permit the extended immersion the method needs. A focus group would generate interaction across participants, yet the actors of interest are dispersed across separate organisations and seldom convene, and the group setting risks suppressing the dissenting views of actors whose institutions hold competing interests, which are exactly the divergences this study seeks to surface. A survey would secure breadth and a distribution of responses, but it would strip away the mechanisms and interpretations on which the research question turns, yielding patterns without explanation and sitting at odds with the critical realist commitment to causal, mechanism-based accounts. The semi-structured interview avoids these constraints while preserving access to actors' situated accounts. Section 3.4.2 turns to how these interviews are sampled and conducted.
