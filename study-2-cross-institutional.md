## 3.4 Research Design and Methods

### 3.4.1 Rationale for Design

This study asks how different institutional contexts influence the misalignment between electoral cycles and megaproject timelines. To answer this question, Study 2 adopts a semi-structured interview design that generates intensive data from actors positioned across the several institutions whose timing norms bear on major infrastructure delivery. The sensitising concepts developed in Study 1, such as entrainment and temporal misfit, serve as an orienting framework for this design rather than as a fixed set of variables to be measured. They direct attention towards where temporal misalignment is likely to surface and how actors account for it, while leaving room for the data to qualify, extend, or displace them. The paragraphs that follow define the semi-structured interview and justify its fit with the study's critical realist and abductive commitments.

A semi-structured interview is a non-standardised, qualitative research interview in which the researcher works from a list of themes and questions to be covered, yet varies them from one interview to the next, omitting some, adding others, and changing their order as the discussion unfolds (Saunders et al., 2023). This sets it apart from a structured interview, which administers a predetermined and standardised set of identical questions, read out in the same way to every respondent to produce quantifiable data (Saunders et al., 2023). The semi-structured form therefore sits between that fixed sequence and the open flow of an unstructured interview, which proceeds freely from an opening question (Albaret & Deas, 2023). Albaret and Deas (2023) described it as a qualitative method that collects data on facts and representations through oral exchange, seeking out respondents' world views in a flexible manner while remaining anchored to a fixed research objective. In practice the researcher prepares a guide of a limited number of essential questions and then uses follow-up and specifying questions to draw participants out, adapting the wording and order of questions to each exchange (Saunders et al., 2023; Albaret & Deas, 2023). The interview is in this sense a social relationship in which the researcher actively shapes the exchange rather than administering a fixed instrument (Albaret & Deas, 2023).

The method also carries limits. It depends on what actors can remember and are willing to articulate, and the interview situation invites them to cast themselves as the protagonists of a tidy narrative and to treat what happened as though it had to happen, tendencies Tavory (2020) termed agency and hindsight bias. It also reaches only those actors who are accessible and willing to take part. For this study these limits are acceptable, because the research question concerns how actors understand and account for temporal misalignment, not a behavioural record independent of their sense-making. Their reasons and interpretations are the very material the analysis requires, and the partiality of any single account becomes evidence about institutional position rather than error to be removed.

The choice of a semi-structured rather than a structured design follows from the nature of the topic. Governance decisions taken under electoral pressure are bound up with political processes that are frequently informal and poorly documented, so much of the relevant reasoning survives only in what actors can be prompted to recount. Albaret and Deas (2023) reported the same difficulty in research on organisations whose work rests on informal discussion and behind-the-scenes operation, where the semi-structured interview is often the only viable route to reconstructing how a course of events or a decision unfolded. A structured interview cannot serve this end, since its fixed and identical questions confine the evidence to what the researcher anticipated and leave no room to follow an unexpected disclosure that surfaces mid-conversation. The semi-structured design removes that constraint, allowing the researcher to probe the unanticipated and to treat surprising disclosures as findings to be developed rather than noise to be set aside.

The semi-structured form fits this study directly. Qualitative interviews are the appropriate tool when the aim is to understand a participant's subjective account of a phenomenon rather than to measure its spread across a population (McGrath et al., 2019), and this study seeks exactly that: how actors perceive, interpret, and respond to temporal misalignment across institutional settings. By keeping to how, who, and when questions, the researcher assembles the building blocks for mechanism-based explanation (Tavory, 2020), which is what a critical realist study needs in order to reason from actors' accounts to the institutional mechanisms behind the misalignment (Fletcher, 2017). The format also suits the study's abductive logic. Abductive interview research, which combines inductive and deductive reasoning, is an established design in organisational studies (Dunwoodie et al., 2022): the sensitising concepts from Study 1 enter as preliminary ideas to be tested and refined against the accounts, not as hypotheses imposed on them. The cross-institutional design holds as well, since a shared set of core questions keeps the interviews consistent across institutions and makes comparison possible, while the latitude to follow each participant lets actors elaborate on what they consider significant. ### 3.4.2 Data Collection

Data collection follows a purposive sampling strategy. Because the study needs actors who have direct experience of temporal misalignment rather than a representative cross-section of a population, random selection would be inappropriate; purposive sampling instead targets participants who can speak to the phenomenon of interest (Dunwoodie et al., 2022; Albaret & Deas, 2023). Three inclusion criteria define the target participants. First, the actor takes part in the megaproject as a member of the inter-institutional temporary organisation, such as a contractor, a project executing agency, or a financing institution. Second, the actor is positioned in an episode where temporal misalignment arises between the electoral cycle and the project timeline. Third, the actor remains tied to the project context at the time of interview, even if their role has since changed. Actors outside the immediate project context, such as those at the macro ministerial level or those who were once involved but no longer are, are considered case by case, according to their relevance to a specific episode of temporal misalignment.

The study sets a minimum target of two actors per institution. This figure is a floor rather than a fixed quota: the sample within any institution may grow through a snowball approach, in which earlier informants point to others, where the data call for further exploration (Albaret & Deas, 2023). The total sample size therefore depends on the number of institutions that make up the inter-institutional temporary organisation and on the analytic needs that emerge during fieldwork. Malterud et al. (2016) provided the rationale for keeping the sample compact. Their concept of information power holds that the more relevant information a sample carries, the fewer participants a study needs, and they identified five conditions that raise information power: a focused study aim, high sample specificity, the use of established theory, a strong interviewer-participant dialogue, and the analysis strategy. Four of these conditions hold here: the aim is narrow and fixed on the mechanisms of temporal misalignment; specificity is high, since participants have directly experienced that misalignment in inter-institutional decision-making; established theory is present in the sensitising concepts carried over from Study 1; and the analysis strategy is cross-institutional comparison. Together these support a smaller sample within each context. Two further sources reinforce this position. Braun and Clarke (2021) argued that reflexive thematic analysis should treat sample size flexibly rather than defer to a rigid notion of data saturation, and Boddy (2016) showed that a small, specific sample can be highly informative when it is framed and justified appropriately.

A pilot interview precedes the extension of fieldwork to the other institutions. Conducted within one institution, the pilot serves several purposes: it tests the interview guide built from the Study 1 sensitising concepts, checks whether the questions reach the mechanisms of temporal misalignment effectively, identifies concepts or wording that need adjusting for different institutional contexts, and prompts an early reflection on the researcher's positionality (Dunwoodie et al., 2022; McGrath et al., 2019). The results feed back into the design in three ways: they refine the interview guide, flag any informant criteria that may need adjustment, and inform a stronger rapport-building strategy for the interviews that follow.

The interview guide develops from the Study 1 sensitising concepts and uses open questions to explore the mechanisms of temporal misalignment; it is a working document rather than a fixed schedule, and it continues to develop as data collection proceeds (Dunwoodie et al., 2022). Interviews take one of two forms. Face-to-face interviews are used where the informant's location and access make them feasible, and online interviews on Zoom or Microsoft Teams are used otherwise, including for interviews that cross national borders. English is the primary language of the interviews. Where an informant is more comfortable in another language, the interview is conducted in that language and the relevant excerpts are translated into English for analysis and reporting, with care taken to preserve their meaning.

With participants' consent, each interview is audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim, which lets the researcher stay present in the conversation rather than divided by note-taking and preserves the detail the analysis depends on (Dunwoodie et al., 2022; McGrath et al., 2019). Transcription begins soon after each interview so that early analytic impressions are not lost (McGrath et al., 2019). The study observes the standard ethical requirements of informed consent, the avoidance of harm, and the protection of participants' privacy, anonymity, and confidentiality (Dunwoodie et al., 2022), which matter particularly here because the actors occupy identifiable institutional positions and speak to politically sensitive episodes. ### 3.4.3 Data Analysis

Analysis draws on reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) as developed by Braun and Clarke (2021). RTA operates here within the study's critical realist frame, so coding attends to the mechanisms that generate temporal misalignment rather than to the surface content of informants' accounts alone. Consistent with RTA, themes are understood as constructions the researcher builds through sustained engagement with the data, not as patterns that lie in the data waiting to be retrieved (Braun & Clarke, 2019). The sensitising concepts carried over from Study 1 give this engagement an initial direction, focusing analytic attention on where and how temporal misalignment is likely to be at work while leaving the analysis open to codes and meanings that those concepts do not anticipate. Reflexive thematic analysis has been applied in megaproject research, including a study of the institutional forces shaping transport megaprojects (Moore et al., 2025).

Analysis will follow the six recursive phases of reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2021):

- familiarisation with the data;
- systematic coding;
- generating initial themes;
- developing and reviewing themes;
- refining, defining, and naming themes; and
- writing up.

The phases are recursive rather than linear, and the analysis will move back and forth among them as understanding develops. Coding is at once a technical and an interpretative act (Ahmed et al., 2025), and it will work at both semantic and latent levels, with weight on latent coding in order to reach the institutional assumptions about time that shape how actors act. Its orientation will be at once deductive, taking direction from the Study 1 sensitising concepts, and inductive, building codes from the data, which RTA treats as a continuum rather than a choice between opposites (Braun & Clarke, 2021). The third phase is named generating initial themes, rather than searching for themes, to mark that themes are produced through this analytic work rather than found already formed in the data (Braun & Clarke, 2019). To preserve contextual meaning, coding will be carried out in the original language of each interview; translation into English will take place at the reporting stage rather than during coding, and institutional terms with no direct English equivalent will be retained in the original language and explained. Coding will be managed in NVivo.

Each institutional context will be coded on its own terms before the analysis brings the contexts together at the stage of theme development, so that each account is read first within its own institutional setting. Comparison across institutions will centre on the convergence and divergence in how actors respond to temporal misalignment. Differences between institutions will be treated as analytic material that speaks to institutional position, not as inconsistencies to be reconciled. Themes will be developed at the level of the dataset as a whole, with institutional position serving as a dimension along which those themes are compared.

A reflexive journal will run throughout the analysis, recording analytic decisions, shifts in interpretation, and translation choices as an audit trail (Ahmed et al., 2025). This reflexivity is built into the coding itself, which Braun and Clarke (2019) described as a continual bending back on oneself, questioning the assumptions brought to interpreting the data. The researcher's positionality, as someone with experience of project management in government, is treated as an interpretive resource to be interrogated rather than a bias to be removed, in keeping with the place RTA gives to researcher subjectivity (Braun & Clarke, 2021). Neither inter-coder reliability nor data saturation is used as a criterion of quality, since both are incoherent with the assumptions of RTA: Braun and Clarke (2021) argued that demonstrating the reliability of coding is illogical within an approach that treats the researcher's interpretation as central, and that saturation belongs to a different analytic tradition. Good reflexive thematic analysis, on this view, is not a matter of accurate or reliable coding or of consensus between coders (Braun & Clarke, 2019). Quality rests instead on the depth of engagement with the data, the transparency of analytic decisions, and ongoing discussion with supervisors.
