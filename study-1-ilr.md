# Study 1 — Integrative Literature Review

## 3.3 Method

### 3.3.1 Rationale of Design

Study 1 employs an integrative literature review (ILR) to address RQ1. Torraco (2016) described the ILR as a form of research that reviews, critiques, and synthesises representative literature on a topic so that new perspectives on that topic emerge. These perspectives arise inductively from the review rather than from a framework fixed at the outset, which makes the method concept-driven and suited to building understanding of a construct (Cronin & George, 2023). The ILR also integrates diverse sources, holding empirical, theoretical, and conceptual work together in one synthesis rather than confining the review to a single type of evidence (Cronin & George, 2023). This orientation separates the ILR from the systematic literature review. Snyder (2019) grouped review methods into systematic, semi-systematic, and integrative types, and held that the review's purpose and question decide which one applies. A systematic review answers a narrow, well-specified question by aggregating comparable studies, and it fits mature topics and standardised interventions where the evidence base is homogeneous (Snyder, 2019). The ILR instead fits concept development, where the literature is dispersed and the aim is to clarify how a construct is understood rather than to estimate an effect (Torraco, 2016).

Temporal misalignment meets the conditions that call for an ILR. The construct is still emerging, and its literature is fragmented across project studies, organisational studies, and political science, with each field naming and theorising temporal phenomena in its own terms. Snyder (2019) argued that a topic conceptualised differently across diverse disciplines resists a full systematic review and calls instead for an approach that combines perspectives across research traditions. An ILR meets that need. It draws these dispersed accounts together and examines how temporal misalignment is conceptualised across them, which a review confined to one discipline or one method could not achieve. The method also admits relevant grey literature, such as government reports and audits, which supplies empirical detail on how temporal misalignment appears in megaproject practice (Kutcher & LeBaron, 2022). This design fits the abductive logic set out in the preceding sections. The review develops the conceptual foundation for the research programme, and Studies 2 and 3 test and refine that foundation against empirical cases.

The use of the ILR is well established in project management and megaproject studies. Watton et al. (2023) employed an ILR to synthesise fragmented research on infrastructure cost drivers, whereas Li et al. (2019) used the method to extend organisational behaviour theory into the megaproject domain. In both cases the ILR served to integrate dispersed literature and build conceptual understanding.

The output of Study 1 is a set of sensitising concepts. Sensitising concepts direct the inquiry by indicating what to look for, while leaving their content open to development through later empirical work. Snyder (2019) noted that for emerging topics an integrative review aims to produce initial or preliminary conceptualisations rather than settled theoretical models, and this framing follows that logic. It matches the emerging state of the literature and the abductive design, and it declines to promise a conceptual framework before the synthesis shows whether one is warranted. The sensitising concepts produced here guide the design, data generation, and analysis of Studies 2 and 3.

### 3.3.2 Data Collection

Data collection begins with a scoping exercise. The scoping search identifies foundational and highly cited sources on temporality and megaprojects, from which the review extracts the keywords that leading scholars use to name the core concepts (Booth & Papaioannou, 2016; Bramer et al., 2018). This step grounds the search terms in the language of the field rather than in terms fixed in advance. The extracted keywords fall into three conceptual blocks: temporality as the construct, conflict or misalignment as the phenomenon, and megaprojects as the context. This three-block structure balances precision and recall, keeping retrieved studies relevant while maintaining broad coverage, and it holds a conceptual logic that links construct, phenomenon, and setting.

The search draws on Scopus and Web of Science, selected together for their coverage across the disciplines this topic spans. Within each block the keywords combine with the Boolean OR operator, and the three blocks combine with AND, so that a record must carry a term from all three blocks to be retrieved. Truncation captures grammatical and spelling variants, as in temporal* and r*ythm*. The third block builds on the validated megaproject keyword list of Denicol et al. (2020), which the review extended with inter-organisational and institutional terms to capture megaprojects framed as multi-actor systems (Brookes et al., 2017; Dille & Söderlund, 2011). A pilot search then refined this block. The term infrastructure* returned a large volume of irrelevant results, such as work on colonisation and workforce issues, so the review removed it. This refinement reflects the iterative character of the integrative review rather than a fixed protocol (Snyder, 2019; Torraco, 2016). Consistent with that character, the search prioritises conceptual coverage and theoretical sensitivity over exhaustive reporting of database-specific Boolean syntax.

Screening applies two sets of criteria. Technical criteria come first and delimit the literature by publication type, language, and disciplinary scope. The review retains peer-reviewed journal articles published in English, and it excludes work from the medical, biological, and physical sciences, where temporality is treated as a physical or biological variable rather than a social one. This boundary keeps the sample aligned with the social-scientific focus of the review. Conceptual criteria follow, operationalised as five sequential screening questions (SQ1 to SQ5) applied to titles and abstracts. SQ1 asks whether the source treats temporality as a social or institutional construct; SQ2 whether it addresses tension, conflict, or disruption between temporal systems; SQ3 whether the context concerns projects, megaprojects, inter-organisational collaboration, or long-duration governance; SQ4 whether the source theorises or examines temporal dynamics rather than describing scheduling technique; and SQ5 whether the argument transfers to megaproject settings. Each question works as a one-way filter: a record excluded at any stage leaves the corpus permanently, while a retained record passes to full-text assessment without presuming inclusion. The database search reported here was carried out during the preparation of this transfer report. The search returned 814 records in Scopus and 1,279 in Web of Science, a total of 2,093. Technical filtering and the removal of duplicates reduced this to a pre-screening corpus of 481 records. Conceptual screening against SQ1 to SQ5 then retained 77 sources. These 77 sources form the corpus that proceeds to full-text analysis.

Two features distinguish this data collection from a strict systematic review. First, the design stays open to relevant grey literature, such as government reports and audits, which supplies empirical detail on how temporal misalignment appears in megaproject practice and helps offset publication bias (Kutcher & LeBaron, 2022). Second, the English-only restriction is a practical limit rather than a conceptual one. English is the dominant language of the databases and disciplines covered, which supports consistent screening and extraction, but the restriction may exclude relevant non-English work, and the review acknowledges this as a limitation (Stockemer & Wigginton, 2019).
