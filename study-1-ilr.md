# Study 1 — Integrative Literature Review

## 3.3 Method

### 3.3.1 Rationale of Design

Study 1 employs an integrative literature review (ILR) to address RQ1. Torraco (2016) described the ILR as a form of research that reviews, critiques, and synthesises representative literature on a topic so that new perspectives on that topic emerge. These perspectives arise inductively from the review rather than from a framework fixed at the outset, which makes the method concept-driven and suited to building understanding of a construct (Cronin & George, 2023). The ILR also integrates diverse sources, holding empirical, theoretical, and conceptual work together in one synthesis rather than confining the review to a single type of evidence (Cronin & George, 2023). This orientation separates the ILR from the systematic literature review. Snyder (2019) grouped review methods into systematic, semi-systematic, and integrative types, and held that the review's purpose and question decide which one applies. A systematic review answers a narrow, well-specified question by aggregating comparable studies, and it fits mature topics and standardised interventions where the evidence base is homogeneous (Snyder, 2019). The ILR instead fits concept development, where the literature is dispersed and the aim is to clarify how a construct is understood rather than to estimate an effect (Torraco, 2016).

Temporal misalignment meets the conditions that call for an ILR. The construct is still emerging, and its literature is fragmented across project studies, organisational studies, and political science, with each field naming and theorising temporal phenomena in its own terms. Snyder (2019) argued that a topic conceptualised differently across diverse disciplines resists a full systematic review and calls instead for an approach that combines perspectives across research traditions. An ILR meets that need. It draws these dispersed accounts together and examines how temporal misalignment is conceptualised across them, which a review confined to one discipline or one method could not achieve. The method also admits relevant grey literature, such as government reports and audits, which supplies empirical detail on how temporal misalignment appears in megaproject practice (Kutcher & LeBaron, 2022). This design fits the abductive logic set out in the preceding sections. The review develops the conceptual foundation for the research programme, and Studies 2 and 3 test and refine that foundation against empirical cases.

The use of the ILR is well established in project management and megaproject studies. Watton et al. (2023) employed an ILR to synthesise fragmented research on infrastructure cost drivers, whereas Li et al. (2019) used the method to extend organisational behaviour theory into the megaproject domain. In both cases the ILR served to integrate dispersed literature and build conceptual understanding.

The output of Study 1 is a set of sensitising concepts. Sensitising concepts direct the inquiry by indicating what to look for, while leaving their content open to development through later empirical work. Snyder (2019) noted that for emerging topics an integrative review aims to produce initial or preliminary conceptualisations rather than settled theoretical models, and this framing follows that logic. It matches the emerging state of the literature and the abductive design, and it declines to promise a conceptual framework before the synthesis shows whether one is warranted. The sensitising concepts produced here guide the design, data generation, and analysis of Studies 2 and 3.

### 3.3.2 Data Collection

Data collection begins with a preliminary scoping exercise. Scoping is needed because temporal misalignment is labelled inconsistently across disciplines, so the search terms have to be grounded in how leading scholars name the core concepts rather than fixed in advance (Booth & Papaioannou, 2016). The scoping keywords are organised into three conceptual blocks: temporality as the construct, conflict or misalignment as the phenomenon, and megaprojects as the context. This three-block structure balances precision and recall, keeping retrieved studies relevant while maintaining broad coverage (Snyder, 2019).

The search then proceeds through six steps.

**Step 1. Preliminary scoping.** The review identifies foundational sources from the relevant sub-fields and extracts the keywords those sources use to name temporality, misalignment, and megaprojects.

**Step 2. Search string development.** The keywords are organised into the three conceptual blocks. Within each block the terms are joined by OR, and the three blocks are joined by AND, so that a retrieved record carries at least one term from every block, in the form [(temporality terms) AND (misalignment terms) AND (megaproject terms)]. The asterisk (*) denotes truncation, which retrieves grammatical and plural variants of a term, as in temporal* for temporal, temporality, and temporally; where placed within a word it also captures spelling variants, as in r*ythm* for rhythm and rythm. A pilot search refined the string by removing terms that retrieved off-topic results, and the full string appears in Appendix [X].

**Step 3. Database search.** The search draws on Scopus and Web of Science and was run on 10 April 2026. The two databases are used together for their coverage across the disciplines this topic spans.

**Step 4. Technical filtering.** The review retains peer-reviewed journal articles published in English and excludes work from the medical, biological, and physical sciences. These fields are excluded because they treat temporality as a physical or biological variable rather than a social one, which falls outside the review's focus.

**Step 5. Duplicate removal.** Records from the two databases are combined and de-duplicated in Zotero. This produced a pre-screening corpus of 481 records.

**Step 6. Conceptual screening.** Five sequential screening questions (SQ1 to SQ5) are applied to titles and abstracts. Each question works as a one-way filter: a record excluded at any stage leaves the corpus permanently, while a retained record passes on without presuming inclusion. This screening retained 77 sources for full-text assessment.

The review also draws on grey literature. The integrative tradition permits grey literature, and it is valuable here because government reports, audits, and official project records hold empirical detail on how temporal misalignment plays out in practice that peer-reviewed articles rarely capture, and their inclusion helps offset publication bias (Kutcher & LeBaron, 2022). Adams et al. (2017) note that grey literature varies in credibility by source expertise and editorial oversight, so Study 1 admits only sources with clear authority and identifiable provenance, and it keeps the English-only restriction applied to the database search (Stockemer & Wigginton, 2019).

Grey literature is located through purposive searching rather than database querying. Two routes are used. The first is targeted searching of the websites and repositories of relevant authorities, such as national audit offices, parliamentary bodies, infrastructure and projects authorities, and international organisations. The second is reference-chasing from the peer-reviewed sample, which surfaces official documents that those articles cite. This purposive approach suits the integrative review, where the aim is to capture relevant empirical detail rather than to cover all available material (Adams et al., 2017).

Grey literature is included against the following criteria:

- **Source authority:** government agencies, international organisations, or established research institutes.
- **Institutional transparency:** identifiable authorship or issuing body.
- **Publication type:** government reports, audit reports, parliamentary inquiries, official project documentation, and official think-tank reports.
- **Language:** English.
- **Date:** an identifiable publication date.
- **Verifiability:** content verifiable through official sources or cross-referenced with the academic literature.
- **Excluded:** blogs, news articles, opinion pieces, social media, and promotional material.

Grey sources are coded with the same framework as the peer-reviewed sample (CC#1 to CC#8), and each inclusion decision is documented to keep the process transparent (Adams et al., 2017).
