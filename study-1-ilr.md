# Study 1 — Integrative Literature Review

## 3.3 Method

### 3.3.1 Rationale of Design

Study 1 addresses RQ1: how is temporal misalignment conceptualised in a megaproject context? This is a question about the state and coherence of a construct, not about the size of an effect or the outcome of an intervention. The design of the study follows from that purpose. The choice of an integrative literature review (IR) rests on three arguments: the nature of the knowledge to be synthesised, the alignment between the review's purpose and the method's analytical logic, and the limitations of the alternative review methods when applied to this problem.

The knowledge on temporal misalignment is fragmented and dispersed across organisation studies, project studies, and institutional theory, and it combines conceptual, theoretical, and empirical work. The construct itself is defined inconsistently across the very sources that are treated as its foundation. Temporal fit and misfit, for example, are anchored to organisation–environment synchronisation in one influential account (Pérez-Nordtvedt et al., 2008) but recast as actor-to-actor institutional matching in another that cites the first as its origin (Dille & Söderlund, 2011); entrainment is defined explicitly in some sources and left undefined in others that nonetheless build on it (Ancona & Chong, 1992). A literature in this state cannot be reduced to a common metric or a pooled estimate. It requires a method that can hold diverse source types together, expose how the construct has been used, and produce a clearer conceptualisation. The integrative review is the review method designed for this task, because it is the only approach that admits the simultaneous inclusion of experimental and non-experimental research alongside theoretical literature in order to understand a phenomenon more fully (Whittemore & Knafl, 2005). It is well suited to concept development and theory building in fragmented and interdisciplinary fields (Cronin & George, 2023; Torraco, 2016).

The second argument concerns the correspondence between purpose and method. Torraco (2016) argues that the value of an integrative review depends on aligning its methods with its purpose, and distinguishes reviews written to reconceptualise a topic from those written to answer descriptive questions or to aggregate findings. RQ1 is a reconceptualisation question: it asks how temporal misalignment is understood, where the accounts diverge, and how the construct can be situated within the megaproject context. The IR supports this purpose through an analytical, concept-driven synthesis rather than the aggregation of results. Extraction in this study therefore records how temporality and misalignment are named, theorised, and attributed across sources, rather than pooling their empirical findings (Snyder, 2019; Toronto & Remington, 2020). This orientation also accommodates theoretical contributions that develop or extend conceptual frameworks and that cannot be appraised with the design-based quality criteria applied to empirical studies (Toronto & Remington, 2020).

The third argument is the inadequacy of the alternative review designs for this problem. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses are the methods of choice where a well-specified clinical or empirical question can be answered by combining studies of similar, if not identical, design (Whittemore & Knafl, 2005). They privilege a narrow sampling frame and hierarchies of evidence, and they cannot integrate the theoretical and conceptual material that carries much of the reasoning about temporality in megaprojects. A narrative review, by contrast, would admit that diversity but without a transparent and systematic procedure for search, screening, and extraction, leaving it more exposed to selective use of the literature. The IR occupies the position between these methods: it retains the breadth of sources that the question demands while imposing an explicit and auditable procedure on the review (Torraco, 2016).

Two further features of the method fit the empirical setting. First, the IR permits the inclusion of relevant grey literature, such as government reports and audits, which helps to mitigate publication bias and to capture temporal dynamics as they appear in megaproject practice rather than only in the academic record (Kutcher & LeBaron, 2022; Oermann & Knafl, 2021). Second, the method's inclusive and concept-sensitive orientation allows sources from adjacent inter-organisational and long-duration project settings to be retained where their argument is analytically transferable to megaprojects, which is necessary because the construct is often theorised outside an explicit megaproject frame.

The IR is not without risk. The combination of diverse sources widens the scope for bias and error at every stage of the review, and its analytical procedures are less codified than those of the systematic review (Whittemore & Knafl, 2005). These risks are treated as design constraints rather than reasons to abandon the method. The review protocol responds to them through a documented search strategy, sequential and explicit screening criteria, a structured coding framework, and an audit trail of analytical decisions, so that the procedure remains transparent and, in principle, replicable.
