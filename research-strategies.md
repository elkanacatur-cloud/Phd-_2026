## 3.3 Research Strategies

The research question that organises the empirical work, how institutional contexts shape the misalignment between electoral cycles and project timelines and how and why that misalignment operates, cannot be answered by a single strategy, because it asks at once about variation across institutions and about the workings of a mechanism within a particular setting. The study therefore divides the empirical labour between two qualitative strategies that answer different parts of the question: a cross-institutional interview study that works broadly, across the range of institutional positions from which actors encounter temporal misalignment, and a case study that works deeply, into the unfolding of one project. This division follows from the study's critical realist commitments, since a generative mechanism has to be recognised as a pattern that recurs across contexts and also traced in the concrete way it operates within a single context, and neither move on its own is sufficient (Fletcher, 2017). Study 1, an integrative review that develops the sensitising concepts, is not an empirical strategy but the conceptual foundation that orients both, indicating where temporal misalignment is likely to be at work without fixing in advance what the empirical strategies will find.

The cross-institutional interview study pursues the variation in how actors positioned in different institutions experience and respond to the misalignment between electoral cycles and the project timeline. This variation lies in the reasons actors give and the interpretations they hold, which the documentary record does not carry, so reaching it depends on asking actors directly rather than on reconstructing decisions from their paper traces (Saunders et al., 2023). What the strategy contributes to the wider research is a set of initial propositions about the mechanisms at work, drawn from the convergences and divergences across institutional positions, which will then be carried forward into the case study. Its reach is also its limit: working across institutions establishes the breadth of the mechanism but cannot show how that mechanism operates within a single, concrete sequence of decisions as it unfolds over time.

The case study pursues an explanation of how and why the mechanisms operate within their institutional and temporal setting, the kind of question a case study answers well through its capacity to give a rich understanding of a phenomenon and the processes at work within its real-life context (Saunders et al., 2023). It closes the limit the interview study leaves open by placing those mechanisms in a chronology of decisions that runs across more than one electoral cycle, where the workings of the misalignment become visible in a way a broad account cannot capture. The two strategies are joined in sequence rather than run side by side, so that the propositions coming out of the interview study will enter the case study as starting points to be examined further and developed, not as conclusions waiting to be confirmed, and where the case departs from those propositions the difference will be treated as material for a fuller explanation rather than as a discrepancy to be resolved. The relationship is cumulative and builds towards a single explanation of temporal misalignment, with the case study as its explanatory centre, so that the two strategies together amount to more than the addition of two separate studies.
